\begin{abstract}
AI-aided cyberworms that adapt their propagation and mimic legitimate traffic threaten mission-critical IoT/IoV/IoD networks, where detectors must remain accurate, lightweight, and robust across distributed sites. This paper proposes CONQUEST, a context-aware, quantum-inspired intrusion detection framework that integrates causal context features, variable-qubit quantum-inspired particle swarm optimization (QPSO) for feature selection, and an LSTM(+VAE) temporal classifier, trained centrally or by horizontal federated learning. \rev{R1.5}\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{Under a leakage-free temporal evaluation on ACI-IoT, CONQUEST achieves 97.13\% accuracy and F1-score, 0.9924 AUC, and the lowest missed-attack rate (3.33\%) among the evaluated pipelines.} \rev{R1.2}\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{Multi-seed ablations show that detection is robust to the QPSO register size $Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$ (Friedman $p\geq0.46$) and that context features and label-aware selection are complementary.} \rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{The deployed detector classifies a traffic window in 2.98\,ms with 541\,MB peak memory.} \rev{R1.8}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{Under IID, quantity-skew, and Dirichlet partitions, federated CONQUEST stays within 0.8\,pp of centralized F1, with a worst-client F1 of 96.37\% under quantity skew.} These results establish CONQUEST as a reproducible, deployable cyberworm detector for mission-critical networks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
The rapid digitalization of mission-critical infrastructures spanning
the Internet of Things (IoT), Internet of Vehicles (IoV), and Internet
of Drones (IoD) has expanded the attack surface of networked
systems~\cite{dritsas, ajakwe2024facets}. AI-aided cyberworms that
adapt autonomously, propagate stealthily, and mimic legitimate
traffic render signature- and rule-based defences increasingly
ineffective~\cite{khezri}. Where delayed or incorrect decisions can
disrupt safety, mobility, or command operations~\cite{amadi2026blockchain},
robust cyberworm detection is both a security requirement and an
operational necessity.

Existing IDS approaches remain limited in this setting.
Signature-driven methods generalize poorly to novel threats, and
statistical anomaly detectors raise many false alarms under noisy,
heterogeneous traffic. Deep learning improves spatio-temporal pattern
recognition~\cite{al202}, but three challenges persist:
(\textit{i})~optimization degrades in high-dimensional feature spaces;
(\textit{ii})~performance is brittle under non-stationary data; and
(\textit{iii})~centralized training conflicts with the privacy,
sovereignty, and regulatory constraints of distributed missions.

Quantum-inspired optimization targets the first
challenge~\cite{ajakwe2025eqai,vivekquantum}: QPSO introduces
probabilistic search dynamics \rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{that have been reported
to improve exploration over classical metaheuristics}. In IDS,
however, its use remains ad hoc: the effect of optimizer capacity is
rarely analyzed, stability is seldom quantified, and
\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{comparisons with classical search at an equal
evaluation budget are missing}. Federated learning (FL) targets the
third challenge by keeping raw traffic local and exchanging only
model updates~\cite{hamadsystematic}; \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{on its
own, FL provides data locality rather than formal privacy, because
updates can leak information unless secure aggregation or
differential privacy is added}. FL-based IDS studies also rarely
report worst-client behavior, convergence stability, or robustness
under realistic non-IID regimes.

This paper presents CONQUEST, a context-aware, quantum-inspired
intrusion detection framework for AI-aided cyberworm detection in
mission-critical networks. It integrates (\textit{i})~causal context
features that capture burst, dormancy, diversity, and transition cues
of cyberworm propagation; (\textit{ii})~variable-qubit QPSO for
\rev{R1.4}\textcolor{red}{budget-bounded selection of a fixed-size
feature subset}; and (\textit{iii})~an LSTM(+VAE) temporal
classifier. Feature optimization runs only during training, so
deployment latency is independent of \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{the
QPSO register size $Q$}. This journal version extends the conference
prototype~\cite{ajakweconquest} with an optimized non-quantum
baseline, a controlled variable-qubit ablation over
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{$Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$}, and a federated evaluation
under IID, label-skew, quantity-skew, and Dirichlet heterogeneity;
\rev{R2.5}\textcolor{red}{Table~\ref{tab:conf_vs_journal} itemizes the
methodological, experimental, and analytical differences.}

\subsection{Key Contributions}
The main contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reproducible context-aware QPSO pipeline:} CONQUEST
    combines causal context features with quantum-inspired feature
    selection under a seed-controlled, leakage-free protocol;
    \rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{QPSO is re-executed for every seed, so the reported
    variance includes the optimizer's own stochasticity.}

    \item \textbf{\rev{R1.1, R1.2}\textcolor{red}{Characterization of the
    register size $Q$:}} \textcolor{red}{We make explicit how $Q$ governs
    the search, from near-random exploration to frozen exploitation,
    and show with multi-seed statistical tests and a search-budget
    analysis that detection is robust to $Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$; the
    operating point $Q^{*}=6$ is set by a pre-declared validation rule.}

    \item \textbf{\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{Attribution and selector guidance:}}
    \textcolor{red}{A factorial ablation, an optimized variance-based
    baseline (OS-CIDS), and classical PSO at an equal budget show that
    context features and label-aware selection are complementary, that
    QPSO and PSO perform on par, and how the choice of selector balances
    missed attacks against false alarms for a given feature budget.}

    \item \textbf{Federated robustness under heterogeneity:}
    \rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{Using worst-client F1,
    inter-client dispersion, and $R_{99}$ on client-specific test sets,
    we show that federated CONQUEST stays within 0.8\,pp of centralized
    F1 under IID, quantity, and Dirichlet heterogeneity and within
    0.9\,pp with up to 20 clients, compare three privacy-compatible
    mask-construction modes, and identify label skew as the most
    challenging regime.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deep Temporal Intrusion Detection}

Intrusion detection for IoT/IIoT has moved from signature-based and
shallow models toward deep architectures that model temporal traffic
behavior, because cyberworm propagation appears as low-intensity,
time-evolving deviations rather than abrupt anomalies. CNN--LSTM and
attention-based models combine local feature extraction with
long-range dependency modeling~\cite{phalaagaehybrid,yuadaptive},
lightweight recurrent autoencoders reduce inference
cost~\cite{yupgtad}, and multi-view flow--packet pipelines reduce
single-view detection errors~\cite{sudyanaflow}. These detectors
typically operate on the full feature space, which raises
computational cost and sensitivity to redundant features and
motivates principled feature selection.

\subsection{Feature Selection via Swarm and Quantum-Inspired Optimization}

Feature selection for IDS is commonly cast as a combinatorial
optimization problem solved by swarm or evolutionary search.
PSO coupled with temporal backbones reduces dimensionality while
preserving discriminative structure~\cite{donkoloptimization};
multi-objective formulations trade detection performance against
subset size~\cite{sezginmulti,raeisientropy}; metaheuristics tune
model hyperparameters~\cite{kishoreimproved}; and wrapper ensembles
extend these ideas to fog and cloud anomaly
detection~\cite{yaseenefficient,bilalevaluation,maryenhancing,shrivashybrid}.
Quantum-inspired sampling has been proposed to strengthen
exploration~\cite{vivekquantum}, but it is usually adopted as a
drop-in replacement, without analysis of how its capacity affects
stability, cost, or detection, \rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{and without
comparison against classical search at an equal evaluation budget}.
These studies also assume centralized access to training data, which
distributed mission environments often cannot provide.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Distributed IDS}

Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative intrusion detection
while keeping raw traffic local~\cite{hamadsystematic}.
Representative systems include agricultural IoT
deployments~\cite{FRIHA202217}, privacy-preserving distributed
designs~\cite{amrosecuring}, federated sequential
detectors~\cite{selvamfederated,anweradvanced}, and unsupervised
VAE/GMM detectors for unknown threats~\cite{camposfederated}. FedProx
has been reported to outperform FedAvg under non-IID partitions on
CICIoT2023~\cite{herlambangfederated}, secure aggregation hardens FL
against collusion and replay attacks~\cite{yaoenhanced}, and
fog-hierarchical architectures improve
scalability~\cite{alsalehheterogeneity,islamprivacy}. These studies
show that FL can reach competitive accuracy without sharing raw data,
but most report global averages under IID or mildly non-IID settings.
In mission deployments, where a single poorly served subsystem can
let a worm spread, worst-client performance matters as much as the
average.

\subsection{Federated Robustness Under Heterogeneous Data}

A separate line of work studies FL under realistic heterogeneity. A
large-scale empirical study reports up to $4.6\times$ quality and
$2.2\times$ fairness degradation under heterogeneous
configurations~\cite{abdelmoniemcomprehensive}. Hierarchical
Dirichlet partitioning extends label-skew simulation with
feature-space clustering~\cite{arisdakessiantwo}; quantity skew has
been addressed with adaptive mutual knowledge
distillation~\cite{gefedamkd} and regularization-adjusted gradient
optimization~\cite{chaaddressing}; and in IDS, personalized FL
improves per-client utility~\cite{almansouradaptive}, while
stability-aware client selection reduces convergence
instability~\cite{vinothinienhancing}. These works seldom report
convergence stabilization, worst-client utility, and inter-client
dispersion together for intrusion detection, and they do not combine
them with capacity-controlled feature selection.

\subsection{Research Gap and Positioning of CONQUEST}

Temporal deep detection, swarm-based feature selection, and federated
learning have each matured, but they are rarely integrated and
evaluated together under deployment constraints.
Table~\ref{tab:rw_qualitative} summarizes representative work.
Relative to it, CONQUEST combines causal context features for
cyberworm dynamics, quantum-inspired selection whose capacity is
analyzed explicitly and compared with classical search, measured
deployment cost, and federated evaluation with tail-aware metrics.
The conference prototype~\cite{ajakweconquest} established QPSO-based
selection with a temporal backbone for centralized detection; this
extension\rev{R2.5}\textcolor{red}{, summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:conf_vs_journal},} adds a variable-qubit ablation over
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{$Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$}, an optimized
non-quantum baseline, and a federated evaluation across IID,
label-skew, quantity-skew, and Dirichlet regimes using $R_{99}$,
$\min_k\,\mathrm{F1}_k$, and $\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$.





\section{\textbf{system overview and design objectives}}
\label{sec:conquest_overview}

IoT/IoV/IoD networks that must meet real-time, memory, and
data-sovereignty constraints. The pipeline is seed-controlled: with
identical data partitions, hyperparameters, and seed, it produces
identical outputs, and its stochastic components are evaluated over
multiple seeds. Table~\ref{tab:acronyms_notations} lists the
notation, and Fig.~\ref{fig:conquest_architecture} shows the

CONQUEST processes raw network traffic in three stages:
context-aware feature construction
(Section~\ref{subsec:feature_pipeline}), variable-qubit QPSO feature
selection (Section~\ref{subsec:qpsomethod}), and temporal binary
classification (Section~\ref{subsec:model_training}).
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{The register size $Q$ does not change the
particle dimension $d$; it controls the exploration--exploitation
balance of the search and is selected on validation data.} QPSO runs
only during training. At deployment, only context-feature construction and
classification run, with the fixed mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}$, so the
inference latency does not depend on $Q$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{infer}}(Q) =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{infer}}^{\text{classical}}.
\label{eq:infer_invariance}
\end{equation}
Training is centralized or federated. In federated mode, $K$ clients
with local datasets $\{\mathcal{D}_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ train
collaboratively with FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(r+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{|\mathcal{D}_k|}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\mathcal{D}_j|}
\,\theta_k^{(r+1)},
\label{eq:fedavg_overview}
\end{equation}
\rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{and only model updates are exchanged, so raw
traffic stays local.} \rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{The design targets are
a per-window inference latency within the budget
$\mathcal{B}_{\rm lat}$, a peak memory $\mathcal{M}\le1$\,GB,} and a
bounded inter-client dispersion $\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$ under non-IID
data, in both training regimes.


%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data Processing and Context-Aware Feature
            Representation}
\label{subsec:feature_pipeline}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Raw flow records $\mathbf{X}(t)$ are mapped by a seed-controlled,
deterministic preprocessing pipeline $\mathcal{P}$ to a unified
representation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}
\mathbf{F}_s \oplus \mathbf{F}_c = \mathbf{Z},
\label{eq:feature_fusion}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{F}_s$ denotes static flow descriptors and
$\mathbf{F}_c$ context-aware features.
\rev{R1.7}\textcolor{red}{$\mathbf{F}_s$ contains the 75 CICFlowMeter
descriptors: ports, protocol, flow duration, packet and byte counts,
packet-length statistics, inter-arrival times, flag counts, and bulk
and activity statistics. Flow identifiers, IP addresses, and
timestamps are excluded to prevent identity shortcuts. The four
\texttt{Idle} fields are also excluded, because in ACI-IoT they store
absolute epoch timestamps and would leak capture time.}
$\mathbf{F}_c$ captures burst activity, dormant intervals, and
protocol state transitions that static descriptors alone do not
reveal. A single global seed controls the
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{measurement} sampling in QPSO and the
weight initialization of the classifier.

\begin{equation}

\label{eq:context_history}
\end{equation}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Variable-Qubit QPSO Feature Selection}
\label{subsec:qpsomethod}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

QPSO learns a sparse binary feature mask
$\mathbf{m}\in\{0,1\}^d$ that gates the normalized input:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{z}_n,\quad
\|\mathbf{m}\|_0 = k \ll d,
\label{eq:mask_gate}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Each of the $P$ particles $i$ holds a
non-negative amplitude vector $\mathbf{a}_i\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{d}$
with $\sum_j a_{i,j}=1$, one amplitude per feature, so the particle
dimension is $d$ for every $Q$. The register size $Q$ enters the
search at three points. First, amplitudes are initialized at the
uniform amplitude of a $Q$-qubit register plus Gaussian noise; after
normalization, only the relative perturbation
$\rho(Q)=2^{Q/2}/(Q^{2}+1)$ matters:}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{a_{i,j}^{(0)} \propto \Bigl|\,2^{-Q/2} +
\tfrac{\xi_{i,j}}{Q^{2}+1}\Bigr|,\quad
\xi_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).}
\label{eq:qpso_init}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Second, at iteration $t$, each particle is
rotated toward its personal-best amplitude $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}$
and the global-best amplitude $\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}$ by an angle whose
upper bound decreases with $Q$:}
\begin{align}
\textcolor{red}{\phi_t} &\textcolor{red}{= \frac{\pi}{2Q}\Bigl(1-\frac{t}{I}\Bigr),}
\label{eq:qpso_angle}\\
\textcolor{red}{\mathbf{a}_i} &\textcolor{red}{\leftarrow \mathbf{a}_i
+ r_1\cos\phi_t\,(\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}-\mathbf{a}_i)
+ r_2\sin\phi_t\,(\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}-\mathbf{a}_i),}
\label{eq:qpso_rotation}
\end{align}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{with $r_1,r_2\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ drawn per
particle and iteration. Third, each amplitude independently
``collapses'' to a fresh random value with a probability that decays
exponentially in $Q$, and the vector is then renormalized:}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{a_{i,j} \leftarrow u_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
\ \text{ with prob. } 2^{-Q},\qquad
\mathbf{a}_i \leftarrow |\mathbf{a}_i| / \|\mathbf{a}_i\|_1 .}
\label{eq:qpso_collapse}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{A mask is obtained by measurement: $k$
distinct feature indices are drawn without replacement with
probabilities $\pi_{i,j}=a_{i,j}^{2}/\sum_{l}a_{i,l}^{2}$, and
$m_{i,j}=1$ for the drawn indices,}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathbf{m}_i = \textsc{Measure}_k(\boldsymbol{\pi}_i),
\qquad \|\mathbf{m}_i\|_0 = k .}
\label{eq:qpso_measure}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Each run returns $\mathbf{m}^{*}$, the
highest-fitness mask measured during the search, as in standard PSO.
Hence $k$ is a fixed design parameter, set to 40 in all experiments,
and $Q$ shapes \emph{which} $k$ features are found, not how many.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:qpso} summarizes the procedure, and
Table~\ref{tab:q_constants} lists the constants set by each $Q$.}
\rev{R1.4}\textcolor{red}{Because a single run's mask depends on its
seed, the deployed detector uses a consensus mask
$\mathbf{m}^{*}_{\rm cons}$: the $k$ features selected most often
across the ten QPSO runs at the selected $Q^{*}$, with ties broken by
the summed fitness of the runs that selected them. All ten runs use
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$ only, so the consensus uses no validation or
test data.}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Variable-qubit QPSO feature
selection. Steps commented with Q are the only ones that depend on
the register size.}}
\label{alg:qpso}
\color{red}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathbf{Z}_{\rm tr}\in[0,1]^{N\times d}$, labels $\mathbf{y}_{\rm tr}$, register size $Q$, particles $P$, iterations $I$, subset size $k$
\Ensure Mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{d}$ with $\|\mathbf{m}^{*}\|_0=k$
\For{$i=1$ to $P$}
  \State $\mathbf{a}_i\gets$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_init}, normalized \Comment{$Q$}
  \State $\mathbf{m}_i\gets\textsc{Measure}_k(\mathbf{a}_i)$; $J_i\gets\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m}_i)$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$
  \State $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}\gets\mathbf{a}_i$; $J_i^{\rm pb}\gets J_i$
\EndFor
\State $i^{\star}\gets\arg\max_i J_i$; $\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}\gets\mathbf{a}_{i^{\star}}$; $\mathbf{m}^{*}\gets\mathbf{m}_{i^{\star}}$; $J^{*}\gets J_{i^{\star}}$
\For{$t=0$ to $I-1$}
  \State $\phi_t\gets\frac{\pi}{2Q}(1-t/I)$ \Comment{$Q$}
  \For{$i=1$ to $P$}
    \State $\mathbf{a}_i\gets$ rotation toward $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb},\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_rotation}
    \State reset each $a_{i,j}$ w.p.\ $2^{-Q}$; normalize, Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_collapse} \Comment{$Q$}
    \State $\mathbf{m}_i\gets\textsc{Measure}_k(\mathbf{a}_i)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_measure}; $J_i\gets\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m}_i)$
    \If{$J_i>J_i^{\rm pb}$}
      \State $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}\gets\mathbf{a}_i$; $J_i^{\rm pb}\gets J_i$
    \EndIf
    \If{$J_i>J^{*}$}
      \State $\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}\gets\mathbf{a}_i$; $\mathbf{m}^{*}\gets\mathbf{m}_i$; $J^{*}\gets J_i$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathbf{m}^{*}$ \Comment{$P(I+1)$ fitness evaluations}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\rev{R1.1}\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{Table~\ref{tab:q_constants} shows
how $Q$ shapes the search. With a single qubit, the rotation bound is
$\pi/2$ and half of all amplitudes are reset at every iteration, so
the search is close to random sampling. As $Q$ grows, the bound
shrinks, the personal-best term dominates the update, and from $Q=7$
onward fewer than one amplitude per particle is reset at each
iteration. $Q$ therefore
moves the search from exploration to exploitation, with a risk of
premature convergence at large $Q$ under a finite budget. Beyond
$Q=8$, resets are rare and the pull toward the global best is weak,
so the search stays close to its initialization. The trained-detector
ablation therefore covers $Q=1$ to $8$, and the budget analysis
extends the range to $Q=10$ to confirm this behavior.}

\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{CONQUEST is quantum-\emph{inspired} and runs
on classical hardware: no $2^{Q}$-dimensional state vector is
simulated, and the particle dimension is $d$ for every $Q$. The term
``qubit'' refers to the register whose uniform amplitude $2^{-Q/2}$
and collapse probability $2^{-Q}$ motivate the three constants
above. $Q$ is thus a single interpretable hyperparameter that sets
the exploration schedule of the swarm, not a measure of model
capacity.}

\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Fig.~\ref{fig:qpso_dynamics} confirms this
behavior empirically. Algorithm~\ref{alg:qpso} is run on
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$ for $Q=1$ to $10$ with ten seeds, without
classifier training, and three regimes appear. At $Q=1$, the
amplitude entropy falls only to 0.75, because half of the amplitudes
are reset at every iteration, and the swarm keeps sampling almost
uniformly. For $Q=4$ to $7$, the entropy falls to 0.25--0.35 within a
few iterations and swarm diversity drops: the search concentrates on
a feature subset. For $Q\ge 9$, the entropy stays at 0.54--0.66,
because small rotations and rare resets barely move the particles.
The best fitness peaks at $Q=4$ and is lower at both extremes,
although the differences are within the seed-to-seed spread.
Returning the best evaluated mask matters most at large $Q$: a fresh
measurement of the final global-best amplitude retains 91\% of the
best fitness at $Q=1$ but only 44--52\% at $Q\ge9$, because those
amplitudes remain spread out and rarely reproduce the best subset.}

\rev{R1.1}\rev{R1.5}\textcolor{red}{Each measured mask is scored by the
Fisher class-separability ratio of its selected features,}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m}) =
\frac{\sum_{c}n_c\,\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_c^{\mathbf{m}}-
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathbf{m}}\|_2^{2}}
{\sum_{c}\sum_{j:m_j=1}\mathrm{Var}_c(z_j)+\epsilon},}
\label{eq:qpsobj}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{where $c\in\{\text{benign},\text{attack}\}$, $n_c$
and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c^{\mathbf{m}}$ are the class count and class
mean of the selected features, $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathbf{m}}$ is
their overall mean, and $\epsilon=10^{-10}$. Personal and global
bests are updated when $\mathcal{J}$ improves. $\mathcal{J}$ is a
filter criterion that needs no classifier training, so one run with
20 particles and 30 iterations costs 620 fitness evaluations.}
\rev{R1.3}\textcolor{red}{One run takes a median of 1.8\,min on the
host of Table~\ref{tab:impl_setup}, and 5.0\,min at the largest
trained budget of 40 particles and 60 iterations.}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{LSTM(+VAE) Temporal Classifier}
\label{subsec:model_training}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The QPSO mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}$ is fixed before classifier training,
so feature selection and model training are decoupled. The masked
input $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{m}^{*}\odot\mathbf{Z}_n$ is passed
to a binary temporal classifier:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathrm{softmax}\bigl(g(\mathbf{u})\bigr),\quad
\mathbf{u} = \textcolor{red}{\begin{cases}
\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, & \text{training},\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}, & \text{inference},
\end{cases}}
\label{eq:classifier}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.11}\textcolor{red}{where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and
$\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2$ are 64-dimensional linear projections of
the final recurrent state and
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$. The
encoder processes windows of $T=10$ flows with an LSTM of 256 units,
two-head self-attention with a residual connection, and a second LSTM
of 128 units, each followed by layer normalization; both LSTMs use
dropout 0.2. The head $g$ consists of dense layers of 64 and 32 ReLU
units, with dropout 0.2 after the first, and a two-unit output layer.
The LSTMs capture the temporal dependencies of cyberworm state
transitions. The variational latent acts only as a stochastic
regularizing bottleneck: it has no reconstruction decoder and is not
used as an anomaly score. At inference, the latent mean is used, so
predictions are deterministic.} The training objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm total} = \textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}
+ \gamma\,D_{\rm KL}\!\left(q(\mathbf{u}|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1:T})\,\|\,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\right)},
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.11}\textcolor{red}{where $\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}$ is the
two-class cross-entropy and $\gamma=10^{-3}$.}
\rev{R1.11}\textcolor{red}{The parameters $\theta$ are optimized with
SGD with Nesterov momentum 0.9, learning rate $10^{-3}$, and batch
size 32. The same optimizer is used in every reported experiment:
all centralized configurations, the $Q$ ablation, the budget and
selector comparisons, and the local updates of all federated clients,
which use batch size 64.} \rev{R1.5}\textcolor{red}{Training runs
for at most 35 epochs. Early stopping monitors the validation loss
with patience 6 and restores the best weights, and the learning rate
is halved after 3 epochs without improvement, down to $10^{-6}$.}
At deployment, no QPSO computation occurs, and inference reduces to
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}=f_\theta(\mathbf{m}^{*}\odot\mathbf{Z}_n)$.

\subsection{Latency-Aware Design and Resource Constraints}
\label{subsec:latency_design}
%% ----------------------------------------------------
\rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{CONQUEST distinguishes two cost
categories, which are measured and reported separately.}

\rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Offline training cost} is incurred
once per model and never on the detection path. It consists of the
QPSO search time $T_{\rm QPSO}(Q)$ and the classifier training time
$T_{\rm train}$, both measured as wall-clock time.}

\rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Online deployment cost} is incurred
for every classified window. The per-window latency is}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{\rm infer} =
\mathcal{L}_{\rm ctx} + \mathcal{L}_{\rm clf},}
\label{eq:latency_decomp}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{where $\mathcal{L}_{\rm ctx}$ is the
incremental context-feature update for a newly arrived flow,
including normalization and masking, and $\mathcal{L}_{\rm clf}$ is
one forward pass of the classifier.}

\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{All timings use \texttt{time.perf\_counter}
on the host of Table~\ref{tab:impl_setup}, with a fixed TensorFlow
thread count per process. The classifier latency is measured at batch
size 1 and reported as the median and 95th percentile over 1{,}000
test windows after 50 warm-up calls. Batched throughput is measured
at batch size 64. For comparison with the conference version, we also
report $T_{\rm infer}^{\rm te}$, the total time to classify all test
windows at batch size 64. The context-feature update
$\mathcal{L}_{\rm ctx}$ is averaged over the test stream and reported
per flow. Memory $\mathcal{M}$ is the peak resident set size of the
inference process. The deployment targets are}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{\rm infer}\le\mathcal{B}_{\rm lat},
\qquad
\mathcal{M}\le\mathcal{M}_{\max}=1\,\text{GB},}
\label{eq:memory_constraint}
\end{equation}
\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{where the latency budget
$\mathcal{B}_{\rm lat}$ depends on the mission and is therefore
reported rather than fixed. The memory target reflects CPU-only
mission-edge gateways that co-host safety services}, where
computation, memory, and energy are tightly
constrained~\cite{amadi2026lightweight}.

\subsection{Centralized Training and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:centralized_method}
%% -----------------------------------------------------
Centralized training establishes the upper-bound detection
performance under full data visibility. 
Three configurations are evaluated with the same optimizer and
stopping rule: S-CIDS, the conference baseline~\cite{ajakweconquest};
OS-CIDS, a static IDS with variance-based feature selection; and
QCIDS, which adds context-aware features and QPSO feature selection.
OS-CIDS and QCIDS therefore differ in two factors at once.
\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{To separate them, a $2\times2$ factorial
ablation crosses context features (off, on) with the selector
(variance, QPSO), all with $k=40$ and the same classifier, so that the
effect of each factor and their interaction can be estimated. A
classical binary PSO selector provides a controlled comparison of
quantum-inspired and classical updates. It uses inertia 0.7,
acceleration coefficients $c_1=c_2=1.5$, a sigmoid position mapping,
top-$k$ decoding, the same Fisher fitness of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpsobj}, and the
same budget of 620 fitness evaluations.} All configurations share
the same data split, preprocessing, and normalization. Model
selection and early stopping use only $\mathcal{D}_{\rm va}$, and all
reported metrics are computed on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$,
\rev{R1.5}\textcolor{red}{as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:data_usage}}:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{m}_{\rm eval} = \bigl\{
&\,\mathrm{Acc},\;\mathrm{F1},\;\mathrm{AUC},\;\mathrm{MSE},\;
  \mathrm{FAR}_{\rm Ben},\nonumber\\
&\,\mathrm{FAR}_{\rm Atk},\;\mathrm{MD}_{\rm Ben},\;
  \mathrm{MD}_{\rm Atk},\;
  \mathcal{T}_{\rm train},\;\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{\rm infer},\;\mathcal{M}}
\bigr\}.
\label{eq:central_metrics}
\end{align}
FAR is the share of benign flows flagged as attack and MD the share
of attacks missed; per-class values are computed from the confusion
matrix. In the
QPSO ablation, every $Q$ uses the same data partition, and results
are reported as mean$\pm$std over ten seeds.
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{Paired comparisons use the two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank test over seeds, with Holm correction when
several settings are compared with the same reference, and
differences across all $Q$ use the Friedman test. Mask stability is
measured by the mean pairwise Jaccard index of the selected feature
sets across seeds.}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Heterogeneity Modeling and Stress-Test Regimes}
\label{subsec:heterogeneity_modeling}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Mission clients differ in traffic volume, class distribution, and
behavioral context because of their roles, duty cycles, and exposure
to attacks. Federated training is therefore evaluated under four
partition regimes of increasing heterogeneity.
\rev{R1.9}\textcolor{red}{The \textit{IID} regime assigns data chunks
to clients at random and serves as the best-case reference. The
\textit{label-skew} regime sorts the chunks by attack fraction and
splits them into $K$ equal contiguous shards, so clients have equal
sizes but class mixes that range from mostly benign to mostly attack.
The \textit{quantity-skew} regime draws client shares from a
Dirichlet distribution with concentration 0.5 and assigns chunks at
random, so client sizes are highly unequal while label proportions
are preserved in expectation.} This regime reflects gateway nodes and
compromised subsystems that generate disproportionate traffic, and it
is the primary stress test because FedAvg weights updates by client
sample size:
\begin{equation}
F(\mathbf{w})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_k}{N}F_k(\mathbf{w}),\quad
F_k(\mathbf{w})=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}_k}
[\ell(\mathbf{w};\mathbf{x},y)],
\label{eq:fedavg_weighted_obj}
\end{equation}
so large clients can dominate the objectives of small ones.
\rev{R1.9}\textcolor{red}{The \textit{Dirichlet} regime draws, for
each class, client shares from $\textsc{Dirichlet}(\alpha\mathbf{1})$
with $\alpha\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,1.0\}$, sweeping from severe heterogeneity
at small $\alpha$ to mild heterogeneity.} Because mission readiness
depends on the weakest clients, every regime is evaluated with global
F1 and AUC together with $R_{99}$, the worst-client F1
$\min_k\mathrm{F1}_k$, and the inter-client dispersion
$\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Federated Learning Protocol and Convergence
            Monitoring}
\label{subsec:federated_protocol_convergence}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

CONQUEST is trained by horizontal federated learning (HFL) with $K$
clients. QPSO feature selection runs before federation on a
server-held proxy set, and the resulting mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}$ is
broadcast to all clients before training begins.
\rev{R1.8}\textcolor{red}{The proxy set is a labelled 5\% subset of
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$, made of evenly spaced contiguous chunks so
that it spans the capture period, and it is withheld from all
clients. It models the small reference capture that a mission
operator typically owns, for example from its own test range or a
public benchmark, and it never contains client traffic. Because one
global mask may suit non-IID clients poorly, three mask-construction
modes are compared. In the default \textit{proxy} mode, QPSO runs on
the proxy set. In the \textit{vote} mode, each client runs QPSO on its
own data and sends only its $k$ selected indices, and the server
keeps the $k$ most frequently selected features. The
\textit{central} mode runs QPSO on the pooled $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$;
it violates data locality and serves only as an oracle reference.}
The mask encodes only feature indices, so no raw traffic or labels
are exposed. Each client $k$ holds
$\mathcal{D}_k=\{(\mathbf{Z}_{k,i},y_{k,i})\}_{i=1}^{N_k}$, built with
the same preprocessing pipeline. The global objective is
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta}\,F(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{N_k}{N}F_k(\theta),
\quad F_k(\theta)=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i=1}^{N_k}
\ell\!\left(f_\theta(\mathbf{Z}_{k,i}),y_{k,i}\right).
\label{eq:hfl_objective}
\end{equation}
In each round $r$, the server broadcasts $\theta^{(r)}$, each
participating client $k\in\mathcal{S}_r$ performs $E=2$ local epochs
on $\mathcal{D}_k$, and the server aggregates the local models with
FedAvg:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(r+1)}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_r}
\frac{N_k}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_r}N_j}
\,\theta_k^{(r+1)}.
\label{eq:fedavg}
\end{equation}


Only model parameters are transmitted; no raw traffic, labels,
or feature vectors are exchanged. \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{We assume
an honest-but-curious server and honest clients. Keeping raw traffic
local guarantees data locality, not formal privacy: plain FedAvg
updates can still leak information about a client's traffic through
membership inference, property inference, or gradient inversion.
Table~\ref{tab:privacy_scope} lists what each party can observe and
how it is protected. Two standard mechanisms close these gaps without
changing the model or the mask. First, \textit{secure aggregation}
with additive pairwise masking lets the server learn only the sum of
the client updates. The masks cancel exactly in the sum, so the
aggregated model and its detection performance are identical to plain
FedAvg; the cost is extra communication and key agreement in each
round. Weighted averaging is preserved by having each client send
$N_k\,\Delta\theta_k$ and $N_k$ through the same protocol. Second,
\textit{client-level differential privacy} (DP-FedAvg) bounds what
the released model reveals about any one client. Each update is
clipped to $\ell_2$ norm $C$, the clipped updates are averaged with
the fixed denominator $K$, and Gaussian noise
$\mathcal{N}(0,(zC/K)^2\mathbf{I})$ is added. With full participation
over $R$ rounds, the R\'enyi-DP accountant gives
$\varepsilon(\delta)=\min_{\alpha>1}\bigl[R\alpha/(2z^2)+
\log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)\bigr]$. DP-FedAvg is evaluated on the
quantity-skew regime with $K=5$, $R=30$, and $\delta=10^{-5}$, using
noise multipliers $z=1$, 3, and 8, which give $\varepsilon=41.3$,
10.4, and 3.5. The clip norm $C=0.215$ is fixed before any private
run as the median client-update norm of the non-private FedAvg runs.}
Blockchain-based mechanisms can additionally secure and audit the
exchange of IoT data among parties that do not fully trust one
another~\cite{amadi2026symmetric}; they complement, but lie outside,
the federated protocol evaluated here.
\rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{Client-level DP is not usable at $K=5$. Over
three seeds, the final global F1 falls from 96.66\% without DP to
$47.2\pm5.1\%$ at $z=1$ with AUC 0.59, $42.8\pm0.0\%$ at $z=3$, and
$36.7\pm10.4\%$ at $z=8$; at $z=3$ and $z=8$ the AUC is 0.50, i.e.,
chance level. Because only five updates are averaged, the noise
standard deviation $zC/K$ is of the same order as the clipped mean
update. Client-level DP is designed for federations of hundreds to
thousands of clients, where this ratio shrinks as $1/K$. For mission
federations with a handful of sites, secure aggregation, which leaves
the aggregated model unchanged, is therefore the practical protection
of individual updates, while formal DP remains a target for larger
federations or for record-level DP-SGD within each site.}

\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{Per-client metrics
evaluate the final global model on a client-specific test set drawn
from $\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$. For each client, up to 2{,}000 test
windows are sampled so that their class and attack-type proportions
match those of the client's training windows, restricted to the
attack types present in $\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$; these types cover at
least 80\% of every client's training mix. This measures how well the
shared model serves each client's own traffic mix, with test sets
large enough to be reliable. The client's held-out chunks are not
used for reporting, because the temporal clustering of ACI-IoT
attacks makes them small and often single-class. Client-count
sensitivity repeats the quantity-skew experiment with $K=5$, 10, and
20.}

\rev{R1.9}\textcolor{red}{SCAFFOLD~\cite{karimireddy2020scaffold} follows
option~II of its original formulation. Server and client control variates $\mathbf{c}$ and
$\mathbf{c}_k$, initialized to zero, correct every local gradient by
$\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_k$, and after local training
$\mathbf{c}_k^{+}=\mathbf{c}_k-\mathbf{c}+(\theta^{(r)}-
\theta_k^{(r+1)})/(\tau\,\eta_{\rm eff})$, where $\tau$ is the number
of local steps. Because local updates use momentum $\mu=0.9$, the
effective step size $\eta_{\rm eff}=\eta/(1-\mu)$ is used; with the
raw $\eta$, the control variates are overestimated tenfold and
training diverges. FLAME~\cite{nguyen2022flame} clusters the client
updates with HDBSCAN, a density-based hierarchical clustering
method~\cite{campello2013hdbscan}, on pairwise cosine distances, with minimum cluster size
$\lfloor K/2\rfloor+1$, and admits the largest cluster. It clips the
admitted updates to the median $\ell_2$ norm of all updates, averages
them, and adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation $10^{-3}$ times
the median norm.} Convergence is measured by
\begin{equation}
R_{99}=\min\bigl\{r:\mathrm{F1}^{(r)}\ge
0.99\,\mathrm{F1}^{(R)}\bigr\},
\label{eq:r90}
\end{equation}
which is normalized to the final F1 and therefore comparable across
regimes and aggregators.
\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{A 99\% threshold is used
because a 90\% threshold is reached in the first round in every regime
and does not discriminate.} \rev{R1.5}\textcolor{red}{The per-round global F1 on
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$ is logged only to describe convergence; the
reported model is always the one after the fixed final round, never a
round selected on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$.} Per-client consistency is
measured by the worst-client F1 $\min_k\mathrm{F1}_k$ and the
inter-client dispersion \rev{R1.9}\textcolor{red}{of the final global
model}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma\!\left(\mathrm{F1}\right)=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\Bigl(\mathrm{F1}_k-\bar{\mathrm{F1}}\Bigr)^2}.
\label{eq:client_var_round}
\end{equation}
Federated training does not change the deployed inference graph:
each client runs the same pipeline with the fixed mask
$\mathbf{m}^{*}$, so the inference latency remains independent of
$Q$.

\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Quantum-inspired versus classical
search.} At an equal budget of 620 fitness evaluations, classical
binary PSO reaches a higher trace-ratio fitness than QPSO, 4919
versus 4401, in less time, 76\,s versus 106\,s, while the resulting
detectors are statistically indistinguishable ($p=0.23$ for F1 and
$p=0.49$ for MD). No quantum advantage is therefore claimed. In
CONQUEST, QPSO is one interchangeable instance of label-aware swarm
selection; its practical merits are a single interpretable control
parameter and robustness to that parameter, not superior accuracy.}

\rev{R1.6}\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Choice of selector.} When
missed attacks are the costlier error, as for self-propagating worms,
label-aware selection with context features is preferable. When false
alarms dominate the operational cost, or labelled data are scarce,
variance ranking on static features gives a comparable F1 with the
fewest false alarms among the competitive pipelines, at negligible
cost and without labels. Context features should not be added to a
label-agnostic selector, and with a very tight feature budget,
variance ranking is again preferable, as shown next.}

0.9\,pp, and quantity skew and IID differ by only 0.11\,pp. Label skew is the one
damaging regime: the global F1 falls to $86.59\pm6.95\%$, the worst
client to 83.50\%, and one attack-dominated client misses 44.5\% of
its attacks. Fig.~\ref{fig:hfl-multianalysis}(a) shows the mechanism:
the global F1 peaks near 94\% around round 9 and then oscillates
downward, as clients that see almost only one class pull the shared
model in opposite directions, a phenomenon known as client drift.
Convergence is fast in the other regimes, with $R_{99}$ between 7.3
and 11.3 rounds.}

\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Aggregation
strategy.} Under quantity skew, FedAvg, SCAFFOLD, and FLAME reach
96.66\%, 96.56\%, and 96.77\% F1. They differ by at most 0.21\,pp,
within one seed standard deviation, and all keep the worst client
above 96.2\%. FedAvg converges fastest, with $R_{99}$ of 7.3 rounds
against 13.7 and 16.3, so it remains the default.}

\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Number of clients.} Splitting the
same training data among 5, 10, and 20 quantity-skewed clients gives a
global F1 of 96.66\%, 96.71\%, and 96.25\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hfl-multianalysis}(c)). Larger federations of smaller
clients converge more slowly, with $R_{99}$ of 7.3, 10.7, and 16.0
rounds, and widen the tail: with 20 clients, the worst client drops to
94.65\% F1 and misses up to 8.03\% of its attacks. CONQUEST therefore
scales to at least 20 clients with a small cost in global F1,
0.41\,pp, but a noticeable cost for the weakest client, which
motivates the tail-aware reporting adopted here.}

\rev{R1.8}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Global mask for non-IID clients.}
The proxy set used for server-side QPSO contains 2{,}750 training
flows, 5\% of $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$, with 54\% attacks of five types,
and it is never given to a client. Building the global mask from this
proxy, from client votes, or from all pooled training data, an oracle
that violates data locality, gives 96.66\%, 97.17\%, and 97.17\%
global F1 under quantity skew, and 96.68\%, 96.60\%, and 97.06\% under
Dirichlet $\alpha=0.1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hfl-multianalysis}(d)). A
global mask built without pooling data therefore costs at most
0.51\,pp relative to the oracle, and the worst client is not
systematically worse off, with a worst-client F1 of 96.2--97.6\%
across all six settings. Clients largely agree on what matters: in
vote mode, 38--43 of the 40 selected features are chosen by at least
three of the five clients. Voting needs no server-held data and,
as Table~\ref{tab:privacy_scope} shows, reveals only feature indices,
which makes it the preferred mode when no suitable proxy data exist.}

\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Cost.} Federated
training takes 169\,s per round on the shared CPU host, or 85\,min for
30 rounds. This cost is incurred only during collaborative training;
the deployed detector is identical to the centralized one and has the
same per-window latency.}

FL-based systems~\cite{FRIHA202217,herlambangfederated}
demonstrate that \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{collaborative} detection is feasible
without raw data sharing, reporting global accuracy up to
99.71\% under IID conditions. However, these studies evaluate
at most two clients, report only global average metrics, and
do not quantify worst-client performance, convergence
stabilization, or behavior under realistic non-IID regimes.
Large-scale empirical analyses~\cite{abdelmoniemcomprehensive}
confirm that heterogeneity can cause up to $4.6\times$ quality
degradation, while FL optimization
studies~\cite{gefedamkd} address quantity-skew on general
vision benchmarks without IDS-specific deployment metrics.
None of these works link non-IID robustness to
mission-facing criteria such as $R_{99}$,
$\min_k\,\mathrm{F1}_k$, or $\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$.
CONQUEST addresses the limitations of both lines of work
within a unified reproducible pipeline. Under centralized
evaluation on ACI-IoT, \rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{CONQUEST achieves
Acc/F1/AUC\,$=$\,97.13\%/97.13\%/0.9924 with the lowest
missed-attack rate of the compared pipelines. A factorial
ablation and an equal-budget classical baseline attribute this to
the combination of context features with label-aware selection,
not to the quantum-inspired search itself, and expose a
missed-attack versus false-alarm trade-off against variance
ranking that centralized IDS studies rarely report.}
\rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{Its deployed detector classifies a window in
2.98\,ms with 541\,MB peak memory on a CPU-only host, independently of
$Q$, which centralized IDS studies seldom measure.}
Under federated deployment,
\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{F1/AUC\,$=$\,0.9666/0.9914 under quantity skew, with
$R_{99}$ of 7.3 rounds, a worst-client F1 of $96.4\%$, and an
inter-client dispersion of $0.7$\,pp, show that the
centralized-federated gap stays below 0.9\,pp outside label skew}
while preserving \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{raw-data locality}, a trade-off that
prior FL-IDS work neither quantifies nor justifies
experimentally.

The conference prototype~\cite{ajakweconquest} reported
97.5\% detection rate and 0.8\% FPR on ACI-IoT under
centralized evaluation, establishing the first

advances this in three ways that are not incremental: (i)~a

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\textcolor{red}{The results above hold under specific conditions,
and six limitations bound the claims that can be drawn from them.}

\textcolor{red}{\textbf{One dataset.} All experiments use ACI-IoT.
Its attacks are clustered in time, so a purely chronological split
would leave the test set entirely benign, and the blocked temporal
split is a compromise between temporal ordering and class coverage.
The test set contains six of the ten attack types, and the class
balance of the 80{,}000-flow subsample, 50\% attacks, is far from the
base rate of an operational network. The absolute F1 values, and the
finding that detection is insensitive to $Q$, therefore need to be
confirmed on other IoT traffic datasets and on longer, naturally
ordered captures before they are generalized.}

\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Static rather than adaptive attackers.} The
paper is motivated by AI-aided cyberworms that adapt their
propagation and mimic legitimate traffic, but ACI-IoT contains fixed
attack implementations. Robustness to an attacker who knows the
selected features and shapes traffic to evade them, for example by
throttling the per-source rates that the context features measure,
is not evaluated.}

\textcolor{red}{\textbf{False alarms.} CONQUEST misses the fewest
attacks, 3.33\%, but raises more false alarms than variance ranking
without context features, 2.24\% versus 1.43\%. On predominantly
benign operational traffic, a false-alarm rate of 2.24\% can still
mean many alerts per hour for an operator. CONQUEST is therefore the
better choice where an undetected worm is the costlier error, and the
decision threshold would have to be calibrated to the alert budget of
the deployment.}

\textcolor{red}{\textbf{No quantum advantage.} QPSO is a classical,
quantum-inspired algorithm, and classical PSO at the same budget of
620 fitness evaluations yields statistically indistinguishable
detectors. The detection gain comes from combining context features
with label-aware selection, not from the quantum-inspired search.
The register-size analysis characterizes how $Q$ shapes the search,
not a performance benefit over classical search.}

\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Label skew and privacy.} Under label skew,
client drift lowers the global F1 to 86.59\% and one client misses
44.5\% of its attacks; drift-aware aggregation and personalization
were not evaluated. Client-level differential privacy reduces the
global F1 to chance level at $K=5$, so the practical protection for
small mission federations is secure aggregation, which was analyzed
but not implemented. Larger federations, where client-level
differential privacy becomes usable, were tested only up to $K=20$
and without it.}

\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Simulated deployment.} Latency and memory
were measured on one CPU-only desktop host, and federated training
was simulated in a single process on that host, without network
delay, client dropout, or partial participation. Measurements on
embedded edge hardware and a networked federation are needed to
confirm the 2.98\,ms per-window latency, the 541\,MB memory, and the
169\,s per round in a fielded system.}





\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented CONQUEST, a context-aware
quantum-inspired intrusion detection framework for AI-aided
cyberworm detection in mission-critical IoT/IoV/IoD networks,
combining seed-controlled context-aware feature representation,
variable-qubit QPSO feature selection, and supervised
LSTM(+VAE) temporal classification.
Three findings are central. First, a controlled ablation over
$Q=1$ to 8 establishes that \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{$Q$ controls
the exploration--exploitation balance of QPSO, with intermediate
values concentrating the search most effectively.}
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{Detection, however, is statistically insensitive to
$Q$ (Friedman $p=0.53$ over ten seeds): every tested setting
yields between 96.95\% and 97.20\% F1, and the validation-selected $Q^{*}=6$
delivers $97.13\%$ accuracy, $97.13\%$ F1, $0.9924$ AUC, and
$0.0246$ MSE under leakage-free centralized evaluation.} \rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{Deployed
on a CPU-only host, it needs 2.98\,ms per window and 541\,MB of
memory, independently of $Q$, because QPSO runs only once during
training.}

Second, \rev{R1.6}\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{context features and label-aware
selection are complementary: each alone lowers F1, while together
they give the lowest missed-attack rate, 3.33\%. QPSO is
statistically equivalent to classical PSO at an equal budget, so
no quantum advantage is claimed, and relative to variance ranking
CONQUEST trades more false alarms for fewer missed attacks, which
favours it where undetected propagation is the costlier error.}
Third, federated evaluation across IID, label-skew,
quantity-skew, and Dirichlet regimes shows that CONQUEST
remains operationally viable without raw data sharing.
\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{Under quantity skew it reaches F1/AUC\,$=$\,96.66\%/0.9914
within 7.3 rounds and keeps the worst client at 96.4\%. It stays
within 0.9\,pp of centralized training under IID, quantity, and
Dirichlet heterogeneity, with up to 20 clients, and with a global
mask built from a small proxy set or from client votes. Label skew is the exception: client drift lowers the global
F1 to 86.6\% and leaves one client missing 44.5\% of its attacks,
which calls for drift-aware aggregation or personalization.}

The unified contribution is the first \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{explicit
characterization of how the QPSO register size controls search
dynamics in IDS feature selection}, integrated with heterogeneity-aware
federated robustness reporting under mission-relevant
metrics that the existing FL-IDS literature does not provide.
Future work will address \textcolor{red}{the limitations in
Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}: validation on further IoT traffic
datasets and against adaptive attackers that shape traffic to evade
the selected features, drift-aware aggregation for label-skewed
federations, measurement on embedded edge hardware,} post-hoc
explainability of the QPSO feature mask, \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{differential privacy at
larger client populations, where the DP noise per client shrinks,} communication-efficient aggregation to
reduce the observed federated
training overhead, and lightweight edge adaptation below
the current \rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{541}\,MB memory envelope.
