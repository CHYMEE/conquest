\begin{abstract}
AI-aided cyberworms that adapt their propagation and mimic legitimate traffic threaten mission-critical IoT/IoV/IoD networks, where detectors must remain accurate, lightweight, and robust across distributed sites. This paper proposes CONQUEST, a context-aware, quantum-inspired intrusion detection framework that integrates causal context features, variable-qubit quantum-inspired particle swarm optimization (QPSO) for feature selection, and an LSTM(+VAE) temporal classifier, trained centrally or by horizontal federated learning. \rev{R1.5}\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{Under a leakage-free temporal evaluation on ACI-IoT, CONQUEST achieves 97.13\% accuracy and F1-score, 0.9924 AUC, and the lowest missed-attack rate (3.33\%) among the evaluated pipelines.} \rev{R1.2}\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{Multi-seed ablations show that detection is robust to the QPSO register size $Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$ (Friedman $p\geq0.46$) and that context features and label-aware selection are complementary.} \rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{The deployed detector classifies a traffic window in 2.98\,ms with 541\,MB peak memory.} \rev{R1.8}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{Under IID, quantity-skew, and Dirichlet partitions, federated CONQUEST stays within 0.8\,pp of centralized F1, with a worst-client F1 of 96.37\% under quantity skew.} These results establish CONQUEST as a reproducible, deployable cyberworm detector for mission-critical networks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
The rapid digitalization of mission-critical infrastructures spanning
the Internet of Things (IoT), Internet of Vehicles (IoV), and Internet
of Drones (IoD) has expanded the attack surface of networked
systems~\cite{dritsas, ajakwe2024facets}. AI-aided cyberworms that
adapt autonomously, propagate stealthily, and mimic legitimate
traffic render signature- and rule-based defences increasingly
ineffective~\cite{khezri}. Where delayed or incorrect decisions can
disrupt safety, mobility, or command operations~\cite{amadi2026blockchain},
robust cyberworm detection is both a security requirement and an
operational necessity.

Existing IDS approaches remain limited in this setting.
Signature-driven methods generalize poorly to novel threats, and
statistical anomaly detectors raise many false alarms under noisy,
heterogeneous traffic. Deep learning improves spatio-temporal pattern
recognition~\cite{al202}, but three challenges persist:
(\textit{i})~optimization degrades in high-dimensional feature spaces;
(\textit{ii})~performance is brittle under non-stationary data; and
(\textit{iii})~centralized training conflicts with the privacy,
sovereignty, and regulatory constraints of distributed missions.

Quantum-inspired optimization targets the first
challenge~\cite{ajakwe2025eqai,vivekquantum}: QPSO introduces
probabilistic search dynamics \rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{that have been reported
to improve exploration over classical metaheuristics}. In IDS,
however, its use remains ad hoc: the effect of optimizer capacity is
rarely analyzed, stability is seldom quantified, and
\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{comparisons with classical search at an equal
evaluation budget are missing}. Federated learning (FL) targets the
third challenge by keeping raw traffic local and exchanging only
model updates~\cite{hamadsystematic}; \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{on its
own, FL provides data locality rather than formal privacy, because
updates can leak information unless secure aggregation or
differential privacy is added}. FL-based IDS studies also rarely
report worst-client behavior, convergence stability, or robustness
under realistic non-IID regimes.

This paper presents CONQUEST, a context-aware, quantum-inspired
intrusion detection framework for AI-aided cyberworm detection in
mission-critical networks. It integrates (\textit{i})~causal context
features that capture burst, dormancy, diversity, and transition cues
of cyberworm propagation; (\textit{ii})~variable-qubit QPSO for
\rev{R1.4}\textcolor{red}{budget-bounded selection of a fixed-size
feature subset}; and (\textit{iii})~an LSTM(+VAE) temporal
classifier. Feature optimization runs only during training, so
deployment latency is independent of \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{the
QPSO register size $Q$}. This journal version extends the conference
prototype~\cite{ajakweconquest} with an optimized non-quantum
baseline, a controlled variable-qubit ablation over
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{$Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$}, and a federated evaluation
under IID, label-skew, quantity-skew, and Dirichlet heterogeneity;
\rev{R2.5}\textcolor{red}{Table~\ref{tab:conf_vs_journal} itemizes the
methodological, experimental, and analytical differences.}

\subsection{Key Contributions}
The main contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reproducible context-aware QPSO pipeline:} CONQUEST
    combines causal context features with quantum-inspired feature
    selection under a seed-controlled, leakage-free protocol;
    \rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{QPSO is re-executed for every seed, so the reported
    variance includes the optimizer's own stochasticity.}

    \item \textbf{\rev{R1.1, R1.2}\textcolor{red}{Characterization of the
    register size $Q$:}} \textcolor{red}{We make explicit how $Q$ governs
    the search, from near-random exploration to frozen exploitation,
    and show with multi-seed statistical tests and a search-budget
    analysis that detection is robust to $Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$; the
    operating point $Q^{*}=6$ is set by a pre-declared validation rule.}

    \item \textbf{\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{Attribution and selector guidance:}}
    \textcolor{red}{A factorial ablation, an optimized variance-based
    baseline (OS-CIDS), and classical PSO at an equal budget show that
    context features and label-aware selection are complementary, that
    QPSO and PSO perform on par, and how the choice of selector balances
    missed attacks against false alarms for a given feature budget.}

    \item \textbf{Federated robustness under heterogeneity:}
    \rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{Using worst-client F1,
    inter-client dispersion, and $R_{99}$ on client-specific test sets,
    we show that federated CONQUEST stays within 0.8\,pp of centralized
    F1 under IID, quantity, and Dirichlet heterogeneity and within
    0.9\,pp with up to 20 clients, compare three privacy-compatible
    mask-construction modes, and identify label skew as the most
    challenging regime.}
\end{itemize}

\section{\textbf{related works}}
\label{sec:related_works}

Cyberworm detection in mission-critical networks must cope with
high-dimensional traffic, training data that cannot be pooled, and
heterogeneous operating conditions. Prior work has advanced these
aspects largely in isolation. This section reviews each line of work
and positions CONQUEST against representative studies
(Table~\ref{tab:rw_qualitative}).

\subsection{Deep Temporal Intrusion Detection}

Intrusion detection for IoT/IIoT has moved from signature-based and
shallow models toward deep architectures that model temporal traffic
behavior, because cyberworm propagation appears as low-intensity,
time-evolving deviations rather than abrupt anomalies. CNN--LSTM and
attention-based models combine local feature extraction with
long-range dependency modeling~\cite{phalaagaehybrid,yuadaptive},
lightweight recurrent autoencoders reduce inference
cost~\cite{yupgtad}, and multi-view flow--packet pipelines reduce
single-view detection errors~\cite{sudyanaflow}. These detectors
typically operate on the full feature space, which raises
computational cost and sensitivity to redundant features and
motivates principled feature selection.

\subsection{Feature Selection via Swarm and Quantum-Inspired Optimization}

Feature selection for IDS is commonly cast as a combinatorial
optimization problem solved by swarm or evolutionary search.
PSO coupled with temporal backbones reduces dimensionality while
preserving discriminative structure~\cite{donkoloptimization};
multi-objective formulations trade detection performance against
subset size~\cite{sezginmulti,raeisientropy}; metaheuristics tune
model hyperparameters~\cite{kishoreimproved}; and wrapper ensembles
extend these ideas to fog and cloud anomaly
detection~\cite{yaseenefficient,bilalevaluation,maryenhancing,shrivashybrid}.
Quantum-inspired sampling has been proposed to strengthen
exploration~\cite{vivekquantum}, but it is usually adopted as a
drop-in replacement, without analysis of how its capacity affects
stability, cost, or detection, \rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{and without
comparison against classical search at an equal evaluation budget}.
These studies also assume centralized access to training data, which
distributed mission environments often cannot provide.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Distributed IDS}

Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative intrusion detection
while keeping raw traffic local~\cite{hamadsystematic}.
Representative systems include agricultural IoT
deployments~\cite{FRIHA202217}, privacy-preserving distributed
designs~\cite{amrosecuring}, federated sequential
detectors~\cite{selvamfederated,anweradvanced}, and unsupervised
VAE/GMM detectors for unknown threats~\cite{camposfederated}. FedProx
has been reported to outperform FedAvg under non-IID partitions on
CICIoT2023~\cite{herlambangfederated}, secure aggregation hardens FL
against collusion and replay attacks~\cite{yaoenhanced}, and
fog-hierarchical architectures improve
scalability~\cite{alsalehheterogeneity,islamprivacy}. These studies
show that FL can reach competitive accuracy without sharing raw data,
but most report global averages under IID or mildly non-IID settings.
In mission deployments, where a single poorly served subsystem can
let a worm spread, worst-client performance matters as much as the
average.

\subsection{Federated Robustness Under Heterogeneous Data}

A separate line of work studies FL under realistic heterogeneity. A
large-scale empirical study reports up to $4.6\times$ quality and
$2.2\times$ fairness degradation under heterogeneous
configurations~\cite{abdelmoniemcomprehensive}. Hierarchical
Dirichlet partitioning extends label-skew simulation with
feature-space clustering~\cite{arisdakessiantwo}; quantity skew has
been addressed with adaptive mutual knowledge
distillation~\cite{gefedamkd} and regularization-adjusted gradient
optimization~\cite{chaaddressing}; and in IDS, personalized FL
improves per-client utility~\cite{almansouradaptive}, while
stability-aware client selection reduces convergence
instability~\cite{vinothinienhancing}. These works seldom report
convergence stabilization, worst-client utility, and inter-client
dispersion together for intrusion detection, and they do not combine
them with capacity-controlled feature selection.

\subsection{Research Gap and Positioning of CONQUEST}

Temporal deep detection, swarm-based feature selection, and federated
learning have each matured, but they are rarely integrated and
evaluated together under deployment constraints.
Table~\ref{tab:rw_qualitative} summarizes representative work.
Relative to it, CONQUEST combines causal context features for
cyberworm dynamics, quantum-inspired selection whose capacity is
analyzed explicitly and compared with classical search, measured
deployment cost, and federated evaluation with tail-aware metrics.
The conference prototype~\cite{ajakweconquest} established QPSO-based
selection with a temporal backbone for centralized detection; this
extension \rev{R2.5}\textcolor{red}{(Table~\ref{tab:conf_vs_journal})}
adds a variable-qubit ablation over
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{$Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$}, an optimized
non-quantum baseline, and a federated evaluation across IID,
label-skew, quantity-skew, and Dirichlet regimes using $R_{99}$,
$\min_k\,\mathrm{F1}_k$, and $\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$.





\section{\textbf{system overview and design objectives}}
\label{sec:conquest_overview}

CONQUEST is a latency-constrained, seed-controlled intrusion
detection framework engineered for mission-critical IoT/IoV/IoD
networks subject to real-time execution, bounded memory, and data
sovereignty constraints. Given identical data partitions,
hyperparameters, and a fixed random seed, the pipeline produces
identical outputs; QPSO ablation results are therefore reported as
mean$\pm$std over repeated seed-controlled runs to account for
stochastic mask sampling. Table~\ref{tab:acronyms_notations}
lists non-standard acronyms and symbols. Fig.~\ref{fig:conquest_architecture}
illustrates the end-to-end architecture.

Let $\mathbf{X}=\{x_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ denote raw network traffic
observations. CONQUEST applies a three-stage transformation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{C}} \mathbf{Z}
           \xrightarrow{\mathcal{Q}_{Q}} \mathbf{Z}^{*}
           \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}} \hat{y},
\label{eq:pipeline}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ is context-aware feature conditioning
(Section~\ref{subsec:feature_pipeline}), $\mathcal{Q}_{Q}$ is
variable-qubit quantum-inspired feature selection
(Section~\ref{subsec:qpsomethod}), and $\mathcal{D}$ is temporal
binary classification (Section~\ref{subsec:model_training}).
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{The register size $Q$ does not change the
dimension of a particle, which is always $d$; it controls the
exploration--exploitation balance of the search
(Section~\ref{subsec:qpsomethod}). CONQUEST selects $Q$
empirically on validation data.} Quantum-inspired computation is strictly
confined to $\mathcal{Q}_{Q}$ during training. At deployment, only
$\mathcal{C}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ execute using the fixed stored mask
$\mathbf{m}^{*}$, ensuring inference latency independent of $Q$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{infer}}(Q) =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{infer}}^{\text{classical}}.
\label{eq:infer_invariance}
\end{equation}
Training executes under centralized or federated regimes. In
federated mode, $K$ clients with heterogeneous local datasets
$\{\mathcal{D}_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ train collaboratively via FedAvg:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(r+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{|\mathcal{D}_k|}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\mathcal{D}_j|}
\,\theta_k^{(r+1)},
\label{eq:fedavg_overview}
\end{equation}
exchanging only model updates to preserve data sovereignty.
CONQUEST is designed to jointly satisfy $\mathcal{L}_{\rm infer}
\leq \mathcal{L}_{\max}$, $\mathcal{M}(Q)\leq 1$\,GB, bounded
$\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$ under non-IID heterogeneity, and
deployability under both execution regimes.

\subsection{Data Processing and Context-Aware Feature
            Representation (Stage~$\mathcal{C}$)}
\label{subsec:feature_pipeline}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Raw traffic $\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ is
transformed into a unified feature representation via a
seed-controlled deterministic preprocessing pipeline:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}
\mathbf{F}_s \oplus \mathbf{F}_c = \mathbf{Z},
\label{eq:feature_fusion}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{F}_s$ denotes static flow descriptors
(\rev{R1.7}\textcolor{red}{ports, protocol, flow duration, packet and byte
counts, packet-length statistics, inter-arrival times, flag
counts, and bulk and activity statistics produced by
CICFlowMeter, $|\mathbf{F}_s|=69$; flow identifiers, IP
addresses, and timestamps are excluded from the feature vector to
prevent identity shortcuts, and so are the four \texttt{Idle}
fields, which in ACI-IoT store absolute epoch timestamps and would
leak capture time}) and $\mathbf{F}_c$ encodes
context-aware features computed offline via fixed sliding-window operators and transition statistics. To ensure end-to-end reproducibility, this global fixed seed governs both the probabilistic \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{measurement} sampling in Stage $\mathcal{Q}_{Q}$ and the stochastic weight initialization of the Stage D temporal classifier. Context features capture burst
activity, dormant intervals, and protocol state transitions that
are not separable under static feature selection alone; no live
behavioral monitoring is performed at runtime.

\rev{R1.7}\textcolor{red}{Table~\ref{tab:context_features} defines
the $|\mathbf{F}_c|=17$ context features exactly. All flows of the
capture are sorted by timestamp; ties at the 1\,s resolution keep
capture order, so ``earlier'' means an earlier position $j<i$ in
the sorted stream. Let $s_i$, $t_i$, $\pi_i$, $p_i$, and $a_i$ be
the source IP, timestamp, protocol, destination port, and
destination IP of flow $i$. The source IP is used only as a
grouping key, never as a feature. For a look-back window $W$,
the history of flow $i$ is}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{H}_W(i)=\{\,j<i:\ s_j=s_i,\ t_j\ge t_i-W\,\},}
\label{eq:context_history}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.7}\textcolor{red}{which excludes flow $i$ itself, and $\mathrm{prev}(i)$
denotes the most recent earlier flow of the same source. Every
feature therefore depends only on past traffic of the same source
and uses no labels. Windows are $W\in\{10,60\}$\,s: 10\,s captures
short scanning and flooding bursts, and 60\,s captures slower
propagation and dormancy patterns; both lengths were fixed before
the experiments and were not tuned. Context features are computed on the full
chronological stream before subsampling, so window statistics
reflect real traffic density. ACI-IoT timestamps are stored as
year--day--month (for example, \texttt{2023-01-11} denotes
1~November~2023) or, for part of the benign traffic, in a
day/month 12-hour format; both are parsed accordingly, which we
verified against the epoch-microsecond values of the
\texttt{Idle} fields. A streaming implementation that keeps, per
source, the flows of the last 60\,s produces values identical to
the batch implementation on real traffic; its per-flow cost is
reported in Section~\ref{subsec:obj2_latency}.}

\subsection{Variable-Qubit QPSO Feature Selection
            (Stage~$\mathcal{Q}_{Q}$)}
\label{subsec:qpsomethod}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Stage $\mathcal{Q}_{Q}$ learns a sparse binary feature mask
$\mathbf{m}\in\{0,1\}^d$ that gates the normalized input:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{z}_n,\quad
\|\mathbf{m}\|_0 = k \ll d,
\label{eq:mask_gate}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Each of the $P$ particles $i$ holds a non-negative
amplitude vector $\mathbf{a}_i\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{d}$ with
$\sum_j a_{i,j}=1$, one amplitude per feature; the particle
dimension is $d$ for every $Q$. The register size $Q$ enters the
search at three points. First, amplitudes are initialized at the
uniform amplitude of a $Q$-qubit register plus Gaussian noise; after
normalization only the relative perturbation
$\rho(Q)=2^{Q/2}/(Q^{2}+1)$ matters (Table~\ref{tab:q_constants}):}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{a_{i,j}^{(0)} \propto \Bigl|\,2^{-Q/2} +
\tfrac{\xi_{i,j}}{Q^{2}+1}\Bigr|,\quad
\xi_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).}
\label{eq:qpso_init}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Second, at iteration $t$ each particle is rotated
toward its personal-best amplitude $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}$ and the
global-best amplitude $\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}$ by an angle whose
upper bound decreases with $Q$:}
\begin{align}
\textcolor{red}{\phi_t} &\textcolor{red}{= \frac{\pi}{2Q}\Bigl(1-\frac{t}{I}\Bigr),}
\label{eq:qpso_angle}\\
\textcolor{red}{\mathbf{a}_i} &\textcolor{red}{\leftarrow \mathbf{a}_i
+ r_1\cos\phi_t\,(\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}-\mathbf{a}_i)
+ r_2\sin\phi_t\,(\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}-\mathbf{a}_i),}
\label{eq:qpso_rotation}
\end{align}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{with $r_1,r_2\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ drawn per
particle and iteration. Third, each amplitude independently
``collapses'' to a fresh random value with a probability that
decays exponentially in $Q$, after which the vector is
renormalized:}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{a_{i,j} \leftarrow u_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
\ \text{ with prob. } 2^{-Q},\qquad
\mathbf{a}_i \leftarrow |\mathbf{a}_i| / \|\mathbf{a}_i\|_1 .}
\label{eq:qpso_collapse}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{A mask is obtained by measurement: $k$ distinct
feature indices are drawn without replacement with probabilities
$\pi_{i,j}=a_{i,j}^{2}/\sum_{l}a_{i,l}^{2}$, and $m_{i,j}=1$ for
the drawn indices,}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathbf{m}_i = \textsc{Measure}_k(\boldsymbol{\pi}_i),
\qquad \|\mathbf{m}_i\|_0 = k .}
\label{eq:qpso_measure}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{The deployed mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}$ is the
highest-fitness mask measured during the search, i.e.\ the
measurement that produced the final global best, as in standard
PSO. Hence $k$ is a fixed design parameter ($k=40$ in all
experiments), and $Q$ shapes \emph{which} $k$ features are found,
not how many. Algorithm~\ref{alg:qpso} summarizes the procedure and
Table~\ref{tab:q_constants} lists the constants set by each $Q$.}
\rev{R1.4}\textcolor{red}{Because a single run's mask depends on its
seed, the deployed detector uses a consensus mask
$\mathbf{m}^{*}_{\rm cons}$: the $k$ features selected most often
across the ten QPSO runs at the selected $Q^{*}$, with ties broken
by the summed fitness of the runs that selected them. All ten masks
are fitted on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$, so the consensus uses no
validation or test information.}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{Variable-qubit QPSO feature selection
(Stage~$\mathcal{Q}_{Q}$). Lines marked $\triangleright Q$ are the only
places where $Q$ enters.}}
\label{alg:qpso}
\color{red}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathbf{Z}_{\rm tr}\in[0,1]^{N\times d}$, labels $\mathbf{y}_{\rm tr}$, register size $Q$, particles $P$, iterations $I$, subset size $k$
\Ensure Mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{d}$ with $\|\mathbf{m}^{*}\|_0=k$
\For{$i=1$ to $P$}
  \State $\mathbf{a}_i\gets$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_init}, normalized \Comment{$\triangleright Q$}
  \State $\mathbf{m}_i\gets\textsc{Measure}_k(\mathbf{a}_i)$; $J_i\gets\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m}_i)$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$
  \State $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}\gets\mathbf{a}_i$; $J_i^{\rm pb}\gets J_i$
\EndFor
\State $i^{\star}\gets\arg\max_i J_i$; $\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}\gets\mathbf{a}_{i^{\star}}$; $\mathbf{m}^{*}\gets\mathbf{m}_{i^{\star}}$; $J^{*}\gets J_{i^{\star}}$
\For{$t=0$ to $I-1$}
  \State $\phi_t\gets\frac{\pi}{2Q}(1-t/I)$ \Comment{$\triangleright Q$}
  \For{$i=1$ to $P$}
    \State $\mathbf{a}_i\gets$ rotation toward $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb},\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_rotation}
    \State reset each $a_{i,j}$ w.p.\ $2^{-Q}$; normalize, Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_collapse} \Comment{$\triangleright Q$}
    \State $\mathbf{m}_i\gets\textsc{Measure}_k(\mathbf{a}_i)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:qpso_measure}; $J_i\gets\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m}_i)$
    \If{$J_i>J_i^{\rm pb}$}
      \State $\mathbf{a}_i^{\rm pb}\gets\mathbf{a}_i$; $J_i^{\rm pb}\gets J_i$
    \EndIf
    \If{$J_i>J^{*}$}
      \State $\mathbf{a}^{\rm gb}\gets\mathbf{a}_i$; $\mathbf{m}^{*}\gets\mathbf{m}_i$; $J^{*}\gets J_i$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathbf{m}^{*}$ \Comment{$P(I+1)$ fitness evaluations}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\rev{R1.1, R1.2}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Interpretation of $Q$.} For $Q=1$, the
rotation bound is $\pi/2$ and half of all amplitudes are reset at
every iteration, so the search is close to random sampling. As
$Q$ increases, the bound shrinks to $\pi/16$ at $Q=8$, the update
is dominated by the personal-best term ($\cos\phi_t\to 1$), and
the expected number of resets per particle and iteration,
$d\,2^{-Q}$, falls below one. $Q$ therefore interpolates between
exploration and exploitation, with a risk of premature convergence
at large $Q$ under a finite budget. Beyond $Q=8$ the collapse
probability is at most $0.2\%$ and the global-best pull
($\sin\phi_t<0.2$) is weak, so the search is effectively frozen
around its initialization; this motivates the tested range
$Q\in\{1,\dots,8\}$. The range is extended to $Q=10$ in the
budget-sensitivity analysis \rev{R1.3}\textcolor{red}{(Section~\ref{subsec:obj1_cyberworm_perf})} to confirm this.}

\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{We stress that CONQUEST is quantum-\emph{inspired}
and runs on classical hardware: no $2^{Q}$-dimensional state vector
is simulated, and the particle dimension is $d$ for every $Q$. The
term ``qubit'' refers to the register whose uniform amplitude
$2^{-Q/2}$ and measurement-collapse probability $2^{-Q}$ motivate
the three $Q$-dependent constants above; $Q$ is therefore a single
interpretable hyperparameter that sets the exploration schedule of
the swarm, not a measure of model capacity.}

\rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Empirical search dynamics.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:qpso_dynamics} verifies this interpretation by
running Algorithm~\ref{alg:qpso} on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$ for
$Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$ and ten seeds, without training any
classifier. Three regimes appear. For $Q=1$, the amplitude entropy
drops only to $0.75$ and stays there, because about half of all
amplitudes are reset at every iteration; the swarm keeps sampling
almost uniformly. For $Q\in\{4,\dots,7\}$, entropy falls to
$0.25$--$0.35$ within a few iterations, and swarm diversity dips
most strongly for $Q\in\{4,5,6\}$, i.e.\ the search concentrates
on a feature subset.
For $Q\ge 9$, entropy decreases slowly and remains near
$0.54$--$0.66$, because the small rotation angle and rare collapses
barely move the particles from their initialization. The best
Fisher fitness found is highest at $Q=4$ ($4836\pm335$) and lower
at both extremes ($4448\pm185$ at $Q=1$, $4426\pm336$ at $Q=10$),
although these differences are within about one standard
deviation. Returning the best evaluated mask matters most in the
exploitative regime: a fresh measurement of the final global-best
amplitude retains $91\%$ of the best fitness at $Q=1$ but only
$44$--$52\%$ at $Q\ge9$, because the
amplitudes at large $Q$ stay close to their spread-out
initialization, so re-sampling them rarely reproduces the best
subset.}

\rev{R1.1, R1.5}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Fitness.} A sampled mask is scored by the
Fisher class-separability ratio of the selected features,}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m}) =
\frac{\sum_{c}n_c\,\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_c^{\mathbf{m}}-
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathbf{m}}\|_2^{2}}
{\sum_{c}\sum_{j:m_j=1}\mathrm{Var}_c(z_j)+\epsilon},}
\label{eq:qpsobj}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{red}{where $c\in\{\text{benign},\text{attack}\}$, $n_c$
and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c^{\mathbf{m}}$ are the class count and
class mean of the selected features, and
$\epsilon=10^{-10}$. Personal and global bests are updated when
$\mathcal{J}$ improves. $\mathcal{J}$ is a filter criterion and
requires no classifier training, so one QPSO run with $P=20$
particles and $I=30$ iterations costs $P(I+1)=620$ fitness
evaluations.} \rev{R1.3}\textcolor{red}{On the CPU host of
Table~\ref{tab:impl_setup}, with three experiments sharing the
machine, it takes a median of $1.7$\,min ($5.0$\,min at the
largest trained budget, $P=40$, $I=60$).}

\rev{R1.5}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Data usage.} Table~\ref{tab:data_usage}

were not tuned on any split. $\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$ is therefore
never used for fitness evaluation, $Q$ selection, weight or
hyperparameter tuning, or early stopping: it is evaluated once per
trained model, after training, to report the final metrics.}

\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Selection of $Q$.} The ablation over
$Q\in\{1,\dots,8\}$ selects the setting with the highest mean
validation F1 over seeds, breaking ties by the lower standard
deviation and lower validation MSE and $\Delta_{\rm bal}$, where}
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\rm bal} =
\bigl|\mathrm{FAR}_{\rm Ben}-\mathrm{MD}_{\rm Ben}\bigr|
+\bigl|\mathrm{FAR}_{\rm Atk}-\mathrm{MD}_{\rm Atk}\bigr|
\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{penalizes asymmetric error modes. Because QPSO is
excluded from the inference path and $k$ is fixed, deployment
latency and memory do not depend on $Q$
(Section~\ref{subsec:latency_design}). The rule selects
$Q^{*}=6$. Test-set results and statistical comparisons
(Table~\ref{tab:qubit_ablation_improved_robust}) show that no
$Q$ in the tested range is significantly better than any other,
so $Q^{*}$ should be read as a reproducible default rather than
an optimum.}

\rev{R1.3}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Search-budget sensitivity.} To test
whether the default budget ($P=20$, $I=30$; 620 evaluations) is
sufficient, and whether the conclusions about $Q$ depend on it, we
(i)~repeat the search alone for $Q\in\{1,\dots,10\}$ at

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{LSTM(+VAE) Temporal Classifier
            (Stage~$\mathcal{D}$)}
\label{subsec:model_training}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The optimal mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}$ from Stage~$\mathcal{Q}_{Q}$
is fixed for classifier training, strictly decoupling feature
optimization from model convergence. The masked input
$\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{m}^{*}\odot\mathbf{Z}_n$ is passed
to a binary temporal classifier:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathrm{softmax}\bigl(g(\mathbf{u})\bigr),\quad
\mathbf{u} = \textcolor{red}{\begin{cases}
\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, & \text{training},\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}, & \text{inference},
\end{cases}}
\label{eq:classifier}
\end{equation}
\rev{R1.11}\textcolor{red}{where $(\boldsymbol{\mu},\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)
\in\mathbb{R}^{64}\times\mathbb{R}^{64}$ are linear projections of
the final recurrent state $h_T$ and
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$.
The recurrent encoder is an LSTM (256 units, returning sequences,
dropout 0.2), followed by layer normalization, two-head
self-attention with a residual connection, layer normalization, a
second LSTM (128 units) and layer normalization, applied over
windows of $T=10$ flows. The head $g$ is Dense(64, ReLU)--Dropout
(0.2)--Dense(32, ReLU)--Dense(2). The LSTM captures temporal
dependencies associated with cyberworm state transitions. The
variational latent acts as a stochastic regularizing bottleneck;
it is not used as an anomaly scorer and has no reconstruction
decoder. At inference, the latent mean is used, so predictions are
deterministic.} The total training objective is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm total} = \textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}
+ \gamma\,D_{\rm KL}\!\left(q(\mathbf{u}|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1:T})\,\|\,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\right)},
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{red}{where $\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}$ is the two-class
cross-entropy and $\gamma=10^{-3}$.} \rev{R1.11}\textcolor{red}{Parameters $\theta$ are
optimized with SGD (learning rate $10^{-3}$, Nesterov momentum 0.9,
batch size 32) for all results reported in this paper: every
centralized configuration (S-CIDS is quoted from~\cite{ajakweconquest}),
the $Q$ ablation, the budget and selector comparisons, and the local
updates of all federated clients (batch size 64). Adam is not used
in any reported experiment; the comparison of SGD, Adam, and
Newton--Hessian updates belongs to the conference
version~\cite{ajakweconquest}, which retained SGD.} \rev{R1.5}\textcolor{red}{Training
runs for at most 35 epochs; early stopping monitors validation
loss with patience 6 and restores the best weights, and the
learning rate is halved after 3 epochs without validation-loss
improvement (minimum $10^{-6}$).} At
deployment, no QPSO computation occurs; inference executes
solely as $\hat{\mathbf{y}}=f_\theta(\mathbf{m}^{*}\odot\mathbf{Z}_n)$,
\rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{which is the basis of~\eqref{eq:infer_invariance};
the corresponding measurements are defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:latency_design}.}

\subsection{Latency-Aware Design and Resource Constraints}
\label{subsec:latency_design}
%% ----------------------------------------------------
\rev{R1.10, R2.4}\textcolor{red}{CONQUEST separates two cost categories that are
measured and reported separately.}

\rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{\textit{(i) Offline training cost}, incurred once
per model and never on the detection path: the QPSO search time
$T_{\rm QPSO}(Q)$ and the classifier training time
$T_{\rm train}$, both measured as wall-clock time on the training
host.}

\rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{\textit{(ii) Online deployment cost}, incurred for
every classified window. The per-window latency is decomposed as}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{\rm infer} =
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}},}
\label{eq:latency_decomp}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{red}{where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}$ is the
incremental context-feature update for a newly arrived flow
(Table~\ref{tab:context_features}), including normalization and
masking, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}$ is one forward pass of
the classifier. QPSO does not appear in~\eqref{eq:latency_decomp},
and because $k$ is fixed, $\mathcal{L}_{\rm infer}$ does not depend
on $Q$~\eqref{eq:infer_invariance}.}

\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Measurement procedure.} All timings use
\texttt{time.perf\_counter} on the CPU-only host of
Table~\ref{tab:impl_setup}, with the TensorFlow thread count fixed
per process. We report: (a) per-window latency at batch size 1,
given as the median and 95th percentile over 1{,}000 test windows
after 50 warm-up calls (ms/window); (b) batched throughput at
$B=64$, in windows/s and its reciprocal in ms/window;
(c) $T_{\rm infer}^{\rm te}$, the total wall-clock time to classify
all $N_{\rm te}$ windows of $\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$ at $B=64$
(seconds, whole test set), reported only for continuity with
earlier tables; and (d) $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}$, amortized
over the test stream (ms/flow). Memory $\mathcal{M}$ is the peak
resident set size of the inference process (MB); training memory
is reported separately. The deployment design targets are}
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{\rm infer}\le\mathcal{B}_{\rm lat},
\qquad
\mathcal{M}\le\mathcal{M}_{\max}=1\,\text{GB},}
\label{eq:memory_constraint}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{red}{chosen for CPU-only mission-edge gateways that
co-host safety services}, where computation, memory, and energy are
tightly constrained~\cite{amadi2026lightweight}.

Section~\ref{subsec:obj2_latency}.}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Heterogeneity Modeling and Stress-Test Regimes}
\label{subsec:heterogeneity_modeling}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Centralized training represents an idealized upper bound; in
practice, mission clients differ in traffic volume, class
distribution, and behavioral context due to role-dependent
behavior, duty-cycle effects, and time-varying adversarial
pressure. To quantify robustness under these conditions,
federated training is evaluated across four heterogeneity
regimes that progressively relax IID assumptions.

The \textit{IID} regime provides the best-case convergence
reference by partitioning data uniformly:
$\mathcal{D}_k\sim\textsc{UniformPartition}(\mathcal{D},n_k)$,
$p_k(y)\approx p(y)$, $n_k\approx N/K$.
\textit{Label-skew} partitions isolate class-distribution effects
by restricting each client to a subset
$\mathcal{Y}_k\subset\mathcal{Y}$ while holding $n_k\approx N/K$
constant. \textit{Quantity-skew} partitions reflect operational
asymmetry gateway nodes and compromised subsystems generate
disproportionate traffic volumes by imposing a heavy-tailed
sample allocation $n_k\propto r_k$, $r_k\sim\textsc{HeavyTail}$,
while preserving global label proportions. This regime is the
primary stress-test because FedAvg weights updates by client
sample size:
\begin{equation}
F(\mathbf{w})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_k}{N}F_k(\mathbf{w}),\quad
F_k(\mathbf{w})=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}_k}
[\ell(\mathbf{w};\mathbf{x},y)],
\label{eq:fedavg_weighted_obj}
\end{equation}
so extreme imbalance allows large clients to suppress
minority-client objectives. \textit{Dirichlet} partitions
($\alpha\in\{0.10,0.30,0.50,1.00\}$) provide a continuous
sweep from severe to mild heterogeneity via
$\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(c)}\sim\textsc{Dirichlet}(\alpha\mathbf{1})$,
with smaller $\alpha$ yielding sharper per-client concentration.
Because mission readiness is governed by tail behavior, all
regimes are evaluated using global F1/AUC alongside
$R_{99}$, $\min_k\mathrm{F1}_k$, and $\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Federated Learning Protocol and Convergence
            Monitoring}
\label{subsec:federated_protocol_convergence}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The heterogeneity regimes above motivate a formal federated
protocol. CONQUEST is deployed in a horizontal federated
learning (HFL) setting with $K$ clients. Feature selection
via QPSO is performed in a pre-federation phase on a
server-held proxy dataset; the resulting mask $\mathbf{m}^{*}$
is broadcast to all clients before federated training begins.
\rev{R1.8}\textcolor{red}{The proxy set is a labelled 5\% subset of
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$, made of evenly spaced contiguous chunks so
that it spans the capture period, and it is withheld from all
clients. It models the small curated reference capture that a
mission operator typically owns, e.g.\ from its own test range or
from a public benchmark, and it never contains client traffic.
Because one global mask may suit non-IID clients poorly, we
compare three mask-construction modes: (i)~\textit{proxy}, the
default above; (ii)~\textit{vote}, in which each client runs QPSO
on its own data and sends only its $k$ selected indices, and the
server keeps the $k$ most frequently selected features; and
(iii)~\textit{central}, an oracle that runs QPSO on the pooled
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$ and is shown only as a reference, since it
violates data locality.}
The mask encodes only feature indices, no raw traffic or
labels are exposed\textcolor{red}{.} Each client $k$ holds
$\mathcal{D}_k=\{(\mathbf{Z}_{k,i},y_{k,i})\}_{i=1}^{N_k}$
constructed via the same deterministic CAP pipeline. The
global objective is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta}\,F(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{N_k}{N}F_k(\theta),
\quad F_k(\theta)=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i=1}^{N_k}
\ell\!\left(f_\theta(\mathbf{Z}_{k,i}),y_{k,i}\right).
\label{eq:hfl_objective}
\end{equation}
In each round $r$, the server broadcasts $\theta^{(r)}$;
each client $k\in\mathcal{S}_r$ performs $E=2$ local epochs
on $\mathcal{D}_k$ to obtain $\theta_k^{(r+1)}$; the server
aggregates via FedAvg:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{(r+1)}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_r}
\frac{N_k}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_r}N_j}
\,\theta_k^{(r+1)}.
\label{eq:fedavg}
\end{equation}

Only model parameters are transmitted; no raw traffic, labels,
or feature vectors are exchanged. \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Threat
model and privacy scope.} We assume an honest-but-curious server
and honest clients. Keeping raw traffic local guarantees data
locality, not formal privacy: plain FedAvg updates can still leak
information about a client's traffic through membership and
property inference or gradient inversion.
Table~\ref{tab:privacy_scope} lists what each party can observe
and which mechanism protects it. Two standard mechanisms close the
gaps without changing the model or the mask. (i)~\textit{Secure
aggregation} (additive pairwise masking) lets the server learn
only the sum of the client updates, so it cannot inspect any
single update. Because the masks cancel exactly in the sum, the
aggregated model, and hence detection performance, is identical
to plain FedAvg; the cost is extra communication and
key-agreement computation per round, not accuracy. Weighted
averaging is preserved by having each client send
$N_k\,\Delta\theta_k$ and $N_k$ through the same protocol. (ii)~
\textit{Client-level differential privacy} (DP-FedAvg) bounds what
the released model reveals about any one client: each update is
clipped to $\ell_2$ norm $C$, the clipped updates are averaged with
the fixed denominator $K$, and Gaussian noise
$\mathcal{N}(0,(zC/K)^2\mathbf{I})$ is added. With full
participation over $R$ rounds, the R\'enyi-DP accountant gives
$\varepsilon(\delta)=\min_{\alpha>1}\bigl[R\alpha/(2z^2)+
\log(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)\bigr]$. We evaluate DP-FedAvg on the
quantity-skew stress regime ($K=5$, $R=30$, $\delta=10^{-5}$) with
noise multipliers $z\in\{1,3,8\}$, i.e.\ $\varepsilon\approx41$,
$10$, and $3.5$. The clip norm $C$ is fixed before any private run
as the median client-update norm of the non-private FedAvg runs.}
Beyond protecting model updates, blockchain-based mechanisms can
additionally secure and audit the exchange of IoT data among
parties that do not fully trust one another~\cite{amadi2026symmetric};
they are complementary to, and outside the scope of, the federated
protocol evaluated here.
\rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Measured cost of client-level DP.}
With the clip norm fixed at $C=0.215$, DP-FedAvg is not usable at
$K=5$: the final global F1 falls from $96.66\%$ without DP to
$47.2\pm5.1\%$ at $z=1$ ($\varepsilon\approx41$, AUC $0.59$),
$42.8\%$ at $z=3$ ($\varepsilon\approx10$), and $36.7\pm10.4\%$ at
$z=8$ ($\varepsilon\approx3.5$, AUC $0.50$), i.e.\ chance level
(three seeds each). Because only five updates are averaged, the
noise standard deviation $zC/K$ is of the same order as the clipped
mean update itself; client-level DP is designed for federations of
hundreds to thousands of clients, where this ratio shrinks as $1/K$.
For mission federations with a handful of sites, secure
aggregation, which leaves the aggregated model unchanged, is
therefore the practical protection of individual updates, while
formal DP remains a target for larger federations or for
record-level DP-SGD within each site.}

\rev{R1.9}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Aggregators.} SCAFFOLD follows option~II of

training diverges. FLAME clusters client updates with HDBSCAN
(minimum cluster size $\lfloor K/2\rfloor+1$) on pairwise cosine
distances, admits the largest cluster, clips the admitted updates
to the median $\ell_2$ norm of all updates, averages them, and adds
Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\lambda$ times the median
norm ($\lambda=10^{-3}$).} Convergence is monitored
using the thresholded stabilization metric:
\begin{equation}
R_{99}=\min\bigl\{r:\mathrm{F1}^{(r)}\ge
0.99\,\mathrm{F1}^{(R)}\bigr\},
\label{eq:r90}
\end{equation}
which remains comparable across heterogeneity regimes and
aggregators by normalizing to terminal performance.
\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{The 90\% threshold of the original submission is
reached in the first round in every regime under the corrected
protocol and therefore no longer discriminates; we use 99\%.}
\rev{R1.5}\textcolor{red}{The per-round global F1 on
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$ is logged only to describe convergence; the
reported federated model is always the one after the fixed final
round, never a round selected on $\mathcal{D}_{\rm te}$.} Per-client
consistency is tracked via worst-case performance
$\min_k\mathrm{F1}_k$ and inter-client dispersion
\rev{R1.9}\textcolor{red}{of the final global model}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma\!\left(\mathrm{F1}\right)=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\Bigl(\mathrm{F1}_k-\bar{\mathrm{F1}}\Bigr)^2}.
\label{eq:client_var_round}
\end{equation}
Federated training does not modify the deployed inference graph;
each client executes the same QCIDS pipeline locally with
fixed $\mathbf{m}^{*}$, preserving the latency guarantee
in~\eqref{eq:infer_invariance}.

\rev{R1.6}\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Overall comparison.}
CONQUEST reaches the highest mean F1 ($97.13\pm0.31\%$) and the
lowest missed-attack rate ($3.33\%$), but its F1 is not
significantly higher than that of OS-CIDS ($96.96\%$, Wilcoxon
$p=0.32$) or of classical PSO with context features ($96.99\%$,
$p=0.23$). The original claim of a $+2.21$\,pp F1 gain over
OS-CIDS, obtained under the leaky protocol of the original
submission, is therefore withdrawn. The pipelines differ instead
in the kind of error they make: CONQUEST misses significantly
fewer attacks than OS-CIDS ($3.33\%$ vs.\ $4.21\%$, $p=0.004$) but
raises significantly more false alarms ($2.24\%$ vs.\ $1.43\%$,
$p=0.002$), and all pipelines with context features or QPSO have
nearly identical AUC ($0.992$--$0.994$), i.e.\ a similar ability
to rank attacks above benign traffic
(Fig.~\ref{fig:radar_centralized}).}

\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Attribution: context features versus
QPSO.} Table~\ref{tab:centralized_compare} separates the two
components. Neither helps on its own. Adding context features to
variance ranking lowers F1 by $0.51$\,pp ($p=0.014$): variance
ranking is label-agnostic and fills 14 of its 40 slots with
high-variance context counts, displacing informative
packet-length features. Replacing variance ranking by QPSO on
static features alone lowers F1 by $1.07$\,pp ($p=0.002$),
mainly through more false alarms ($4.95\%$). Combined, however,
context features and QPSO raise F1 by $1.23$\,pp over QPSO alone
and by $0.68$\,pp over variance ranking with context (both
$p\le0.004$); the interaction ($+1.74$\,pp, $p=0.002$) is the
dominant effect. The gain of CONQUEST is therefore attributable to
the combination of context-aware representation with label-aware
selection, which keeps about eight context features alongside
complementary static features (Table~\ref{tab:selected_features}).}

\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Quantum-inspired versus classical
search.} At an equal budget of 620 fitness evaluations, classical
binary PSO reaches a higher trace-ratio fitness than QPSO
($4919$ vs.\ $4401$) in less time ($76$\,s vs.\ $106$\,s), while
the resulting detectors are statistically indistinguishable
(F1 $96.99\%$ vs.\ $97.13\%$, $p=0.23$; MD $p=0.49$). We therefore
make no claim of a quantum advantage. In CONQUEST, QPSO is one
interchangeable instance of label-aware swarm selection; its
practical merits are a single interpretable control parameter
$Q$ (Sec.~\ref{subsec:qpsomethod}) and robustness to that
parameter (Table~\ref{tab:qubit_ablation_improved_robust}), not
superior accuracy.}

\rev{R1.6}\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Which selector when.} The
results suggest the following guidance. When missed attacks are
the costlier error, as for self-propagating worms, label-aware
selection (QPSO or PSO) with context features is preferable. When
false alarms dominate the operational cost, or labelled data are
scarce, variance ranking on static features gives a comparable F1
with the fewest false alarms among the competitive pipelines, at
negligible selection cost and without labels. Context features
should not be added to a label-agnostic selector.} \rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{When
the feature budget is very tight ($k\approx10$), variance ranking is
again preferable, as shown next.}

\label{subsec:obj2_latency}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{Because detection is insensitive to $Q$
(Table~\ref{tab:qubit_ablation_improved_robust}), this subsection
examines whether $Q$ affects deployment cost and whether the
selected operating point satisfies mission-edge constraints.}

\rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{Table~\ref{tab:latency_profile_qcids} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:deploy_cost} report the deployment cost, measured in
isolation on the CPU-only host with the units and procedure of
Section~\ref{subsec:latency_design}. Classifying one window of
$T=10$ flows takes CONQUEST a median of $2.98$\,ms at batch size 1
(95th percentile $4.55$\,ms); classified in batches of 64, a window
costs $0.25$\,ms, i.e.\ about 4{,}000 windows per second. Updating
the context features, normalizing, and masking a newly arrived flow
adds $16\,\mu$s, and the whole test set of 11{,}544 windows is
classified in $3.23$\,s. The inference process peaks at $541$\,MB,
within the 1\,GB budget. These costs are practically the same for
every pipeline ($2.9$--$3.5$\,ms per window; $541$--$658$\,MB) and
for $Q=1,6,8$ and $k=10,20,40$: QPSO is not executed at deployment,
and the classifier hardly changes with the input width ($758$k--$789$k
parameters), which confirms~\eqref{eq:infer_invariance}
empirically. QPSO is a training-time cost of about $1.8$\,min per
run on the shared host ($0.4$\,min at $k=10$), incurred once before
training; the consensus mask requires ten such runs.}

\rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{\textit{Relation to the original latency figures.} The
$3.60$\,s reported as inference time in the original submission
was the total time to classify the whole test set (about 12{,}000
windows), not a per-detection latency; it corresponds to roughly
$0.3$\,ms per window and is consistent with $T_{\rm infer}^{\rm te}$
above. The $61.2$\,ms end-to-end latency, the latency-budget share

path; Eq.~\eqref{eq:latency_decomp} replaces it.}

\subsection{Federated Robustness Under Heterogeneous Mission Deployments}
\label{subsec:obj3_heterogeneity}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------
Centralized training is an idealized upper bound; in practice,
raw traffic cannot be aggregated across organizational,
operational, or jurisdictional boundaries. The key question
is not whether federated deployment degrades performance, but
whether CONQUEST remains operationally viable under realistic
data heterogeneity. Fig.~\ref{fig:hfl-multianalysis} and
Table~\ref{tab:fl_performance_extended} jointly address this.

\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{One configuration for all federated
results.} The original submission mixed configurations (8 rounds
with one local epoch for the aggregator comparison, 15 rounds for
the heterogeneity sweep, and 60 rounds for the extended run),
which is why it reported two different FedAvg results for quantity
skew (F1 $0.910$ and $0.869$). All federated experiments have been
re-run with one configuration ($R=30$, $E=2$, batch 64, full
participation, proxy mask unless stated, three seeds), so every row
of Table~\ref{tab:fl_performance_extended} is directly comparable.}

\rev{R1.9}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Heterogeneity.} Under IID,
quantity-skew, and Dirichlet partitions, the global F1 lies between
$96.34\%$ and $96.94\%$, within $0.2$--$0.8$\,pp of centralized
CONQUEST ($97.13\%$); for the quantity-skew stress regime the gap is
$0.47$\,pp. These regimes also serve every client well: the worst
client keeps F1 $\ge96.4\%$ and the inter-client dispersion stays
below $0.9$\,pp. The earlier observation that quantity skew
outperforms IID is not reproduced; the two differ by $0.11$\,pp.
Label skew is the one damaging regime: the global F1 falls to
$86.59\pm6.95\%$, the worst client to $83.50\%$, and one
attack-dominated client misses $44.5\%$ of its attacks.
Fig.~\ref{fig:hfl-multianalysis}(a) shows the mechanism: the global
F1 peaks near $94\%$ around round 9 and then oscillates downward as
clients that see almost only one class pull the shared model in
opposite directions (client drift). Convergence is fast in the
benign regimes ($R_{99}$ of 7--11 rounds).}

\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Aggregation strategy.} Under quantity skew,
FedAvg ($96.66\%$), SCAFFOLD ($96.56\%$), and FLAME ($96.77\%$)
differ by at most $0.21$\,pp, within one seed standard deviation,
and all keep the
worst client above $96.2\%$. FedAvg converges fastest ($R_{99}=7.3$
versus $13.7$ and $16.3$ rounds), so it remains the default. The
original submission's collapse of SCAFFOLD (F1 $0.522$) is not
reproduced: that result came from a server-momentum approximation
that was labelled SCAFFOLD, whereas the revision implements the
published SCAFFOLD and FLAME algorithms
(Section~\ref{subsec:federated_protocol_convergence}).}

\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Number of clients.} Splitting the same
training data among $K=5$, $10$, and $20$ quantity-skewed clients
gives global F1 of $96.66\%$, $96.71\%$, and $96.25\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hfl-multianalysis}(c)). Larger federations of
smaller clients converge more slowly ($R_{99}$ of 7.3, 10.7, and
16.0 rounds) and widen the tail: at $K=20$ the worst client drops
to $94.65\%$ F1 and misses up to $8.0\%$ of its attacks. CONQUEST
therefore scales to at least 20 clients with a modest cost in
global F1 ($-0.41$\,pp) but a noticeable cost for the weakest
client, which motivates the tail-aware reporting adopted here.}

\rev{R1.8}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Global mask for non-IID clients.} The
proxy set used for server-side QPSO contains 2{,}750 training flows
($5\%$ of $\mathcal{D}_{\rm tr}$, $54\%$ attacks, five attack
types) and is never given to a client. Building the single global
mask from this proxy, from client votes, or from all pooled
training data (an oracle that violates data locality) gives
$96.66\%$, $97.17\%$, and $97.17\%$ global F1 under quantity skew
and $96.68\%$, $96.60\%$, and $97.06\%$ under Dirichlet
$\alpha=0.1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hfl-multianalysis}(d)). A global mask
built without pooling data therefore costs at most about
$0.5$\,pp relative to the oracle, and the worst client is not
systematically worse off (Min-F1 $96.2$--$97.6\%$ across all six
settings). Clients largely agree on what matters: in vote mode,
38--43 of the 40 selected features are chosen by at least three of
the five clients. Voting needs no server-held data and reveals only
feature indices (Table~\ref{tab:privacy_scope}), which makes it the
preferred mode when no suitable proxy data exist.}

\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Cost.} Federated training costs about
170\,s per round on the shared CPU host (about 85\,min for 30
rounds). This cost is incurred only during collaborative training;
the deployed detector is identical to the centralized one and has
the same per-window latency
(Section~\ref{subsec:obj2_latency}).}

\subsection{Comparative Analysis with State-of-the-Art}
\label{subsec:comparative_analysis}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:comparative_analysis} positions CONQUEST
against representative IDS and FL approaches. Since methods
are evaluated on different datasets, direct numerical accuracy
comparison is not valid; the analysis contrasts
deployment-oriented capabilities feature-selection stability,
federated robustness, worst-client reliability, and
latency--memory compliance for which CONQUEST provides the
most comprehensive characterization.
Hybrid deep-learning detectors report high accuracy on their
respective benchmarks: CNN--LSTM with
attention~\cite{phalaagaehybrid} achieves up to 99.87\% on
WIoTSN, and a transformer-based IIoT
detector~\cite{yuadaptive} reaches 99.6\%/99.2\% Acc/F1 at
10.8\,ms inference with 37.2M parameters.
Optimization-enhanced pipelines~\cite{donkoloptimization,shrivashybrid}
exceed 99\% on NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15. These results are
obtained under centralized assumptions with no evaluation of
optimizer-capacity stability, heterogeneous client behavior,
or deployment constraints. They establish performance
ceilings on well-studied benchmarks but do not address the
reproducibility, bounded overhead, or mission-edge feasibility
requirements central to this work.

FL-based systems~\cite{FRIHA202217,herlambangfederated}
demonstrate that \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{collaborative} detection is feasible
without raw data sharing, reporting global accuracy up to
99.71\% under IID conditions. However, these studies evaluate
at most two clients, report only global average metrics, and
do not quantify worst-client performance, convergence
stabilization, or behavior under realistic non-IID regimes.
Large-scale empirical analyses~\cite{abdelmoniemcomprehensive}
confirm that heterogeneity can cause up to $4.6\times$ quality
degradation, while FL optimization
studies~\cite{gefedamkd} address quantity-skew on general
vision benchmarks without IDS-specific deployment metrics.
None of these works link non-IID robustness to
mission-facing criteria such as $R_{99}$,
$\min_k\,\mathrm{F1}_k$, or $\sigma(\mathrm{F1})$.
CONQUEST addresses the limitations of both lines of work
within a unified reproducible pipeline. Under centralized
evaluation on ACI-IoT, \rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{CONQUEST achieves
Acc/F1/AUC\,$=$\,97.13\%/97.13\%/0.9924 with the lowest
missed-attack rate of the compared pipelines. A factorial
ablation and an equal-budget classical baseline attribute this to
the combination of context features with label-aware selection,
not to the quantum-inspired search itself, and expose a
missed-attack versus false-alarm trade-off against variance
ranking that centralized IDS studies rarely report.}
\rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{Its deployed detector classifies a window in
2.98\,ms with 541\,MB peak memory on a CPU-only host, independently of
$Q$, which centralized IDS studies seldom measure.}
Under federated deployment,
\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{F1/AUC\,$=$\,0.9666/0.9914 under quantity skew, with
$R_{99}{\approx}7$ rounds, a worst-client F1 of $96.4\%$, and an
inter-client dispersion of $0.7$\,pp, show that the
centralized-federated gap stays below 0.9\,pp outside label skew}
while preserving \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{raw-data locality}, a trade-off that
prior FL-IDS work neither quantifies nor justifies
experimentally.

The conference prototype~\cite{ajakweconquest} reported
97.5\% detection rate and 0.8\% FPR on ACI-IoT under
centralized evaluation, establishing the first
cyberworm-focused CONQUEST result. \rev{R2.5}\textcolor{red}{The
two works differ in method, experiments, and analysis
(Table~\ref{tab:conf_vs_journal}). Methodologically, the extension
replaces random splitting of pooled data with a blocked temporal,
training-only protocol, defines the context features and the role
of $Q$ exactly, and adds federated training. Experimentally and
analytically,} the journal extension
advances this in three ways that are not incremental: (i)~a
controlled variable-qubit ablation over $Q\in\{1,\dots,8\}$
that \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{makes the role of $Q$ explicit and
characterizes its effect on search dynamics and detection}
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{and shows that detection is statistically
insensitive to $Q$, so the operating point is set by a pre-declared
validation rule}; (ii)~\rev{R1.6}\textcolor{red}{a factorial ablation
and an equal-budget classical PSO baseline that attribute the
detection gain to the combination of context features and
label-aware selection rather than to quantum-inspired search};
and (iii)~federated
evaluation under four heterogeneity regimes with
mission-facing deployability metrics that prior
QPSO-based IDS work does not provide. Together, these
extensions transform a centralized feasibility demonstration
into a deployment-oriented framework with quantified
robustness bounds.





\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented CONQUEST, a context-aware
quantum-inspired intrusion detection framework for AI-aided
cyberworm detection in mission-critical IoT/IoV/IoD networks,
combining seed-controlled context-aware feature representation,
variable-qubit QPSO feature selection, and supervised
LSTM(+VAE) temporal classification.
Three findings are central. First, a controlled ablation over
$Q\in\{1,\dots,8\}$ establishes that \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{$Q$ controls
the exploration--exploitation balance of QPSO, with intermediate
values concentrating the search most effectively.}
\rev{R1.2}\textcolor{red}{Detection, however, is statistically insensitive to
$Q$ (Friedman $p=0.53$ over ten seeds): every tested setting
yields about $97\%$ F1, and the validation-selected $Q^{*}=6$
delivers $97.13\%$ accuracy, $97.13\%$ F1, $0.9924$ AUC, and
$0.0246$ MSE under leakage-free centralized evaluation.} \rev{R1.10}\rev{R2.4}\textcolor{red}{Deployed
on a CPU-only host, it needs 2.98\,ms per window and 541\,MB of
memory, independently of $Q$, because QPSO runs only once during
training.}

Second, \rev{R1.6}\rev{R2.3}\textcolor{red}{context features and label-aware
selection are complementary: each alone lowers F1, while together
they give the lowest missed-attack rate ($3.33\%$). QPSO is
statistically equivalent to classical PSO at an equal budget, so
no quantum advantage is claimed, and relative to variance ranking
CONQUEST trades more false alarms for fewer missed attacks, which
favours it where undetected propagation is the costlier error.}
Third, federated evaluation across IID, label-skew,
quantity-skew, and Dirichlet regimes shows that CONQUEST
remains operationally viable without raw data sharing.
\rev{R1.8}\rev{R1.9}\rev{R2.2}\textcolor{red}{Under quantity skew it reaches F1/AUC\,$=$\,96.66\%/0.9914
within about 7 rounds, keeps the worst client at 96.4\%, and stays
within 0.9\,pp of centralized training across IID, quantity, and
Dirichlet heterogeneity and with up to 20 clients
and with a global mask built from a small proxy set or from client
votes. Label skew is the exception: client drift lowers the global
F1 to 86.6\% and leaves one client missing 44.5\% of its attacks,
which calls for drift-aware aggregation or personalization.}

The unified contribution is the first \rev{R1.1}\textcolor{red}{explicit
characterization of how the QPSO register size controls search
dynamics in IDS feature selection}, integrated with heterogeneity-aware
federated robustness reporting under mission-relevant
metrics that the existing FL-IDS literature does not provide.
Future work will address post-hoc explainability of the
QPSO feature mask, \rev{R2.1}\textcolor{red}{differential privacy at
larger client populations, where the DP noise per client shrinks,} communication-efficient aggregation to
reduce the observed federated
training overhead, and lightweight edge adaptation below
the current \rev{R1.10}\textcolor{red}{541}\,MB memory envelope.
